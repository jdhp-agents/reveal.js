Planning with a simulator

A Markov decision process $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$. We do not need $P$ explicitly, only a generative model (a simulator):

$$ (s, a) \;\longmapsto\; (s', r) \qquad \text{with } s' \sim P(\cdot \mid s, a), \;\; r = R(s, a, s') $$

Online planning (receding horizon): at each real time step $t$,

  1. spend a budget of $n$ simulations starting from the current state $s_t$,
  2. play $a_t \approx \arg\max_a Q^*(s_t, a)$ in the real environment,
  3. observe $s_{t+1}$ and start again.

No value function or policy over the whole state space: only the states reachable from $s_t$ matter.

Examples: Go and chess (AlphaGo, AlphaZero), Atari games with an emulator, robot motion planning, combinatorial optimization, …

Cadre : un MDP dont on ne connaît pas forcément P, mais qu'on peut simuler (modèle génératif). Planification en ligne : à chaque pas de temps réel, on dépense n simulations depuis l'état courant pour choisir l'action, on la joue, et on recommence.
Let's start with the setting. We have a Markov decision process, but we don't need its transition probabilities. We only need a simulator, also called a generative model. We give it a state and an action, and it returns a next state and a reward. [click] We use it for online planning. At each real time step, we run n simulations from the current state. Then we play the best action we found, in the real environment. We observe the new state, and we start again. [click] So we never compute a policy for the whole state space. Only the states reachable from here matter. [click] This is how AlphaGo plays Go, and how AlphaZero plays chess. It also works for Atari games with an emulator, for robot motion planning, or for combinatorial optimization.

Exhaustive search does not scale

$\displaystyle V^*(s) = \max_{a} Q^*(s,a), \qquad Q^*(s,a) = \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, \big[ R(s,a,s') + \gamma\, V^*(s') \big]$

Unrolling this recursion over a horizon $H$ costs $O\big( (|\mathcal{A}| \cdot |\mathcal{S}'|)^H \big)$ nodes, with $|\mathcal{S}'|$ the number of possible next states. Go: about $250$ moves per position, games of $150$ moves $\Rightarrow$ $250^{150} \approx 10^{360}$.

La récursion de Bellman (expectimax) développée sur un horizon H explose exponentiellement. Figure : depuis la position de tic-tac-toe utilisée plus loin, l'arbre de jeu complet (à gauche) et l'arbre construit par MCTS en 200 itérations (à droite), asymétrique.
Why not just compute the best action exactly? The Bellman equation is a recursion: a max over the actions, then an average over the next states, and again. If we unroll it over a horizon H, the tree grows exponentially with H. For Go, with about 250 moves per position and 150 moves per game, that's ten to the power 360 nodes. On the left, a tiny example: a tic-tac-toe position with only six empty cells. We will come back to it in the second part. The exhaustive tree already has more than a thousand nodes, for 520 possible games. [click] On the right, the tree built by MCTS after 200 iterations: only 188 nodes. It is asymmetric: deep on the promising moves, shallow elsewhere. The size of a node shows how often it was visited.

Monte Carlo evaluation and the bandit problem

Monte Carlo: estimate the value of an action by the mean return of simulated episodes (rollouts) — no need to expand the whole tree.

How to share the budget between the actions? A multi-armed bandit: $K$ arms with unknown mean rewards $\mu_a$; pull arms to maximize the total reward $\Rightarrow$ exploration vs exploitation.

UCB1 (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer, 2002):

$$ a_t = \arg\max_a \; \underbrace{\bar{X}_a}_{\text{exploitation}} \; + \; \underbrace{c \sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{N_a}}}_{\text{exploration}} $$

For rewards in $[0, 1]$ and $c = \sqrt{2}$, each suboptimal arm is pulled only $O(\ln n)$ times in $n$ pulls: the regret grows logarithmically.

Évaluation Monte Carlo par moyenne de rollouts ; répartition du budget = problème de bandit. UCB1 : moyenne empirique + bonus d'exploration qui décroît avec N_a et croît (lentement) avec t. La figure déroule UCB1 sur 4 bras de Bernoulli (vraies moyennes en pointillés rouges).
Instead of expanding the whole tree, we can sample. The value of an action is estimated by the mean return of random simulations, called rollouts. But which action should we simulate next? This is the multi-armed bandit problem: exploration versus exploitation. The classic answer is UCB1: the empirical mean, plus an exploration bonus. The bonus is large when an arm was rarely pulled. On the right, four arms, with their true means in dashed red, unknown to the algorithm. [click] First, each arm is pulled once: dark blue is the mean, light blue is the bonus. The framed arm is the one UCB1 pulls next. [click × 4] Look at arm 3: its mean is zero, but its bonus grows with time, so it gets a second chance. [click × 3] After 3,000 pulls, the bonuses are small, and the best arm, arm 2, gets most of the pulls. In theory, each bad arm is pulled only a logarithmic number of times.

MCTS: the four phases of an iteration

Tree policy: inside the tree, each node is a bandit, solved with UCB1 — UCT, Upper Confidence bounds applied to Trees (Kocsis & Szepesvári, 2006).
Default policy: beyond the tree, random moves until the end of the episode.
Anytime: stop whenever the budget is exhausted and play the most visited action at the root.

Les 4 phases : sélection (descente avec UCB1), expansion (ajout d'un nœud), simulation (rollout aléatoire), rétropropagation (mise à jour de N et Q sur le chemin). Tree policy vs default policy ; algorithme anytime.
MCTS combines these two ideas: a tree, and a bandit at each node of the tree. Each iteration has four phases. First, selection: we go down the tree, and at each node, UCB1 chooses the child. [click] Second, expansion: at the bottom of the tree, we add one new node. [click] Third, simulation: from this new node, a random rollout until the end gives a return G. [click] Fourth, backpropagation: we go back up, and G updates N and Q along the path. [click] And we repeat this n times. Inside the tree, UCB1 is the tree policy: this version of MCTS is called UCT. Beyond the tree, the random moves are the default policy. We can stop at any time and play the most visited action: MCTS is anytime.

A stochastic environment: FrozenLake

Let's see these four phases in action. We start with a stochastic environment. There, the same action can lead to different states.

FrozenLake de Gymnasium, version glissante : chaque action a 3 issues équiprobables. Depuis la case 10, DOWN va vers le but mais tombe dans le trou 11 avec probabilité 1/3 ; LEFT s'éloigne du but mais ne peut jamais tomber dans un trou.
Our stochastic environment is FrozenLake, from Gymnasium. The agent must reach the goal G, without falling into a hole H. The reward is 1 at the goal, and 0 everywhere else. But the ice is slippery. The agent moves in the intended direction only one time out of three. Otherwise, it slips sideways, to the left or to the right of this direction. We plan from state 10, the red dot, near the goal, with gamma equal to 0.95. [click] So, what should we do? DOWN looks natural: it goes towards the goal. But one time out of three, the agent slips to the right, into the hole. [click] LEFT moves away from the goal. But look: whatever happens, it can never fall into a hole. Keep this in mind: we will see which one MCTS prefers.

The search tree of a stochastic MDP

Two kinds of nodes alternate:

  • state nodes $v$ (decision): the agent chooses $a$ with UCB1
  • action nodes $(v, a)$ (chance): the simulator draws $s' \sim P(\cdot \mid s, a)$

An action node gets one child per distinct sampled outcome $s'$. The outcomes are drawn with probability $P(s' \mid s, a)$, so the mean $Q(v, a)$ weights them correctly without ever computing $P$.

Statistics: $N(v)$, $N(v, a)$ and $Q(v, a)$ = mean return observed after playing $a$ in $v$.

Dans un MDP stochastique, l'arbre alterne nœuds état (on choisit l'action avec UCB1) et nœuds action, qui sont des nœuds de chance (le simulateur tire l'état suivant). Un nœud action a autant de fils que d'issues distinctes tirées. Statistiques : N(v), N(v,a), Q(v,a).
In a stochastic environment, the search tree has two kinds of nodes. A state node is a decision node. Here, the agent chooses the action, with UCB1. Below it, an action node is a chance node. Here, nobody chooses: the simulator draws the next state. So an action node can have several children, one for each outcome we have sampled. On the figure, LEFT and DOWN each have three possible outcomes. And DOWN can end in the hole, in red. Do we need to know the probabilities P? No: the outcomes are drawn with their true probabilities. So the mean Q of v, a weights them correctly, automatically. The state node stores N of v, and the action node stores N of v, a, and Q of v, a.

MCTS on FrozenLake, step by step

for $i = 1, \dots, n$ do
$v \leftarrow v_0$ ; $\;\text{path} \leftarrow (\,)$
while $v$ is not terminal do
if all actions of $v$ have been tried then
$a \leftarrow \arg\max_{a} \; Q(v,a) + c \sqrt{\ln N(v) \,/\, N(v,a)}$
else $a \leftarrow$ an untried action ; add node $(v,a)$
$(s', r) \leftarrow \textsf{simulator}(s(v), a)$ ▷ $s' \sim P(\cdot \mid s(v), a)$
$\text{path} \leftarrow \text{path} + (v, a, r)$
if $s'$ is not yet a child of $(v,a)$ then
add node $s'$ under $(v,a)$ ; $v \leftarrow s'$ ; break
$v \leftarrow$ the child $s'$ of $(v,a)$
$G \leftarrow \textsf{rollout}(s(v))$ ▷ random policy, $G = \sum_t \gamma^t r_t$
for $(v, a, r) \in \text{path}$, from last to first do
$G \leftarrow r + \gamma\, G$
$N(v) \leftarrow N(v) + 1$ ; $\;N(v,a) \leftarrow N(v,a) + 1$
$Q(v,a) \leftarrow Q(v,a) + \big(G - Q(v,a)\big) / N(v,a)$
return $\arg\max_a N(v_0, a)$

Backpropagation of discounted returns

For a path $(v_0, a_0, r_0), \dots, (v_{L-1}, a_{L-1}, r_{L-1})$ ending at the leaf $v_L$, the update $G \leftarrow r + \gamma G$ gives, at depth $d$:

$$ G_d = \sum_{k=d}^{L-1} \gamma^{k-d}\, r_k \; + \; \gamma^{L-d}\, G_L \qquad \text{with } G_L = \sum_{t \geq 0} \gamma^t\, r^{\text{rollout}}_t $$

i.e. the discounted return of the whole simulated episode from $v_d$: tree part + rollout part.

Incremental mean — no need to store the returns:

$$ Q_N = Q_{N-1} + \frac{1}{N}\big(G - Q_{N-1}\big) \iff Q_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} G^{(i)} $$

$N(v) = \sum_a N(v, a)$: each iteration through $v$ increments exactly one of its actions.

Here every return lies in $[0, 1]$ (a single terminal reward $\leq 1$), as UCB1 with $c = \sqrt{2}$ assumes. Otherwise: normalize the returns, or tune $c$.

La mise à jour G ← r + γG reconstruit le retour actualisé de tout l'épisode simulé depuis chaque nœud du chemin (partie arbre + partie rollout). Moyenne incrémentale. N(v) = somme des N(v,a). Retours dans [0,1] : hypothèse de UCB1 avec c = √2.
Let's look at the backpropagation more closely. We go up the path, and at each step, G becomes r plus gamma times G. So at depth d, G is the discounted return of the whole simulated episode, from this node. It has two parts: the rewards collected inside the tree, and the return of the rollout. [click] Q is updated as an incremental mean. It is exactly the average of all the returns, but we don't need to store them. [click] And N of v is the sum of the N of v, a, over its actions. Each visit of v increments exactly one of its actions. [click] One more detail: UCB1, with c equal to root two, assumes rewards between 0 and 1. Here it is true, because the only reward is 1, at the goal. In general, we must normalize the returns, or tune the constant c.

Convergence at the root

  • $Q(s_0, a)$ stays far below $Q^*(s_0, a)$: it averages the returns of an evolving policy — exploration inside the tree, random rollouts beyond it.
  • Yet the most visited action converges to the optimal one, LEFT, long before the values do: the decision is right before the estimates are.
Courbes en fonction de n (échelle log) : les Q estimés convergent lentement et restent biaisés vers le bas (moyenne des retours d'une politique qui explore + rollouts aléatoires, et aucun partage entre nœuds représentant le même état). Mais la part des visites de LEFT (action optimale) finit par dominer : la décision est bonne bien avant les valeurs.
Let's zoom on the root, as a function of n, on a log scale. On the left, the estimate Q of each root action, with the same colors as before. [click] The dashed lines are the exact values Q star, computed by value iteration. The estimates are biased low, even after twenty thousand iterations. Why? Q is the average return of the policy that MCTS actually played. Inside the tree, it still explores, and beyond the tree, it plays randomly. And the same state appears in many nodes, which all learn separately. [click] On the right, the share of visits. DOWN and LEFT compete for a long time, then LEFT takes over, after a few thousand iterations. So the decision is right long before the values are. And for planning, the decision is what matters.

Large or continuous outcome spaces

With continuous next states, every simulation samples a new outcome $s'$: chance nodes get one child per visit, and the tree never grows deeper.

Progressive widening (Coulom, 2007; Couëtoux et al., 2011): at the chance node $(v, a)$, add a new child only while $$ |\text{children}(v, a)| \leq k \, N(v, a)^{\alpha}, \qquad \alpha \in (0, 1) $$ otherwise, go down to one of the existing children (in proportion to its visits). Double progressive widening applies the same rule to the actions.

Open-loop MCTS: nodes are action sequences; the states are re-simulated at each iteration instead of being stored (Perez Liebana et al., 2015).

Si l'espace des états suivants est grand ou continu, chaque tirage crée un nouveau fils et l'arbre ne s'approfondit jamais. Progressive widening : le nombre de fils d'un nœud de chance croît comme k N^α. Variante open-loop : les nœuds sont des séquences d'actions.
In FrozenLake, each action has at most three outcomes. But what if the next state is continuous, like a position in a physics simulation? Then every simulation gives a brand new outcome. Chance nodes get one new child per visit, and the tree never grows deeper. [click] The standard fix is progressive widening. We add a new child only while the number of children is below k times N to the power alpha. Otherwise, we go down to one of the existing children. So the tree widens slowly, and goes deeper on the outcomes it already has. Double progressive widening applies the same rule to the actions. [click] Another option is open-loop MCTS: the nodes store action sequences, not states. The states are simulated again at each iteration.

Part 2

A deterministic two-player zero-sum game: tic-tac-toe

Partie 2 : MCTS sur un jeu déterministe à deux joueurs et à somme nulle.
Now, a very different setting: a two-player game. The moves are deterministic, but there is an opponent, who wants us to lose.

From MDPs to game trees

Deterministic: $s' = f(s, a)$ — no chance node; the action and the next state are one and the same node (node = position, edge = move).

Two players, zero-sum: the players alternate; what one wins, the other loses: $r_{\text{O}} = 1 - r_{\text{X}}$ (win $1$, draw $\frac{1}{2}$, loss $0$).

Minimax (negamax form) — value for the player to move: $$ V(s) = \max_{a} \big[ 1 - V(f(s, a)) \big] $$ and $V(s)$ = reward of the player to move if $s$ is terminal.

Jeu déterministe : l'état suivant est une fonction de (s, a), donc le nœud action et le nœud état suivant fusionnent (nœud = position, arête = coup). Deux joueurs à somme nulle : r_O = 1 − r_X. Récursion minimax sous forme negamax.
What changes in the tree? First, the game is deterministic: a move from a position always gives the same position. So we don't need chance nodes. The action and the next state become one single node: a node is a position, an edge is a move. Second, there are two players, who alternate, and the game is zero-sum. A win is worth 1, a draw one half, a loss 0. What X wins, O loses. So each node stores N, and W, the total reward of the player who moved into it. The exact solution is minimax. In this negamax form, my value is the best, over my moves, of one minus the value of my opponent.

The position: O to play

X occupe deux coins opposés, O le centre, O doit jouer. Le flat Monte Carlo (moyenne de parties aléatoires) préfère les coins, parce qu'un X aléatoire trouve rarement la fourchette. Le minimax dit le contraire : jouer un coin perd (X bloque et fait une fourchette), seuls les bords tiennent la nulle.
Here is our position. X has two opposite corners, O has the center, and it is O's turn. There are six possible moves: which one would you play? [click] Flat Monte Carlo plays twenty thousand random games after each move, and averages the results. Its favorite moves are the two corners. [click] But minimax says that the corners lose! After a corner, O threatens a line, so X must block. And this block creates a fork: two threats at once, and O cannot stop both. Random players rarely find this sequence. Only the edges hold the draw. Can MCTS do better than flat Monte Carlo?

MCTS on tic-tac-toe, step by step

for $i = 1, \dots, n$ do
$v \leftarrow v_0$
while $v$ is not terminal and all moves of $v$ were tried do
$v \leftarrow \arg\max_{v' \in \text{children}(v)} \; \dfrac{W(v')}{N(v')} + c \sqrt{\dfrac{\ln N(v)}{N(v')}}$
if $v$ is not terminal then
$v \leftarrow$ a new child of $v$, for an untried move
$z \leftarrow$ result of a random playout from $v$
while $v \neq \varnothing$ do
$N(v) \leftarrow N(v) + 1$
$W(v) \leftarrow W(v) + \text{reward}_{p(v)}(z)$ ▷ $1$ / $\tfrac{1}{2}$ / $0$
$v \leftarrow \text{parent}(v)$
return the move to the most visited child of $v_0$

$p(v)$: the player who made the move leading to $v$; $W(v)/N(v)$ is its win rate, so at $v$ the player to move maximizes over the children of $v$.

Déroulé pas à pas (27 clics). Pas de nœud de chance ; W/N de chaque nœud est compté pour le joueur qui y a mené (couleur). Itérations 1–6 : un fils par coup ; le coin bas-gauche gagne son premier playout. 7 : UCB1 le choisit. 9 : UCB1 choisit un autre coup grâce au bonus. 19 : descente sur deux niveaux (X répond au mieux pour X). Après 19 itérations, MCTS jouerait le coin… perdant.
Same exercise on tic-tac-toe, with a shorter code: there is no chance node. [click × 4: iteration 1] We add one move of O, play a random game until the end, and back up the result. Each node counts the reward of the player who moved into it: red for O, blue for X. [click × 7: iterations 2 to 6] One new child per iteration, and only the bottom-left corner wins its random game. [click × 4: iteration 7] Now all moves are tried, and UCB1 picks this corner, the best mean. [click × 5: iterations 8 and 9] At iteration 9, UCB1 picks another move: its exploration bonus wins. [click × 7: iterations 14 and 19] At iteration 19, we go down two levels. Below the corner, it is X's turn: UCB1 now maximizes X's win rate. In the backup, the same result counts 1 for the winner, and 0 for the loser. After 19 iterations, MCTS would play the corner… the losing move!

Tic-tac-toe: the root after $n$ iterations

Même exécution prolongée. Le coin, d'abord favori, perd ses visites dès que l'arbre est assez profond pour que X y trouve sa fourchette ; au-delà d'environ 500 itérations MCTS joue un bord et les W/N tendent vers les valeurs minimax (½ pour les bords, 0 pour les coins). Contrairement au flat Monte Carlo, MCTS converge vers la décision minimax.
Let's run this search longer: after 19 iterations, the bottom-left corner is the favorite. [click] After a hundred iterations, the corner still leads, but its win rate drops, from 0.86 to 0.67. Why? The tree is now deep enough below the corner, and X starts to find the fork. [click] After three hundred iterations, an edge, the top, takes the lead. [click] After a thousand, the two corners lose their visits: about ten percent each. [click] After five thousand, the four edges share the visits, and their win rate is close to one half, their minimax value. The corners are still at 0.34: their average includes the early visits, and the visits where X explores weaker moves. So, unlike flat Monte Carlo, MCTS converges to the minimax decision. The playouts are still random, but the tree corrects them where it matters.

Part 3

Synthesis, theory and modern variants

Partie 3 : synthèse, théorie et variantes modernes.
Let's put the two versions side by side. Then we will see what the theory says, and how modern programs like AlphaZero use MCTS.

Two variants of the same algorithm

Stochastic MDP (FrozenLake)

for $i = 1, \dots, n$ do
$v \leftarrow v_0$ ; $\;\text{path} \leftarrow (\,)$
while $v$ is not terminal do
if all actions tried: $a \leftarrow \arg\max_a \mathrm{UCB}(v, a)$
else $a \leftarrow$ an untried action ; add node $(v,a)$
$(s', r) \leftarrow \textsf{simulator}(s(v), a)$ ▷ chance node
$\text{path} \leftarrow \text{path} + (v, a, r)$
if $s'$ is new: add node $s'$ ; $v \leftarrow s'$ ; break
$v \leftarrow$ the child $s'$ of $(v,a)$
$G \leftarrow \textsf{rollout}(s(v))$
for $(v, a, r) \in \text{path}$, last to first do
$G \leftarrow r + \gamma\, G$ ▷ discounted return
$N(v) \mathrel{+}= 1$ ; $\;N(v,a) \mathrel{+}= 1$
$Q(v,a) \mathrel{+}= \big(G - Q(v,a)\big)/N(v,a)$
return $\arg\max_a N(v_0, a)$

$\mathrm{UCB}(v, a) = Q(v, a) + c \sqrt{\ln N(v) / N(v, a)}$

Two-player zero-sum game (tic-tac-toe)

for $i = 1, \dots, n$ do
$v \leftarrow v_0$
while $v$ is not terminal and fully expanded do
$v \leftarrow \arg\max_{v' \in \text{children}(v)} \mathrm{UCB}(v')$ ▷ player to move
if $v$ is not terminal: $v \leftarrow$ new child of $v$ ▷ move = state
$z \leftarrow$ result of a random playout from $v$
while $v \neq \varnothing$ do
$N(v) \mathrel{+}= 1$
$W(v) \mathrel{+}= \text{reward}_{p(v)}(z)$ ▷ $z$ or $1 - z$
$v \leftarrow \text{parent}(v)$
return the move to the most visited child of $v_0$

$\mathrm{UCB}(v') = W(v')/N(v') + c \sqrt{\ln N(v) / N(v')}$

Here are the two versions, side by side. The skeleton is exactly the same: selection, expansion, simulation, backpropagation. The highlighted lines are the differences. On the left, the stochastic case needs a chance node. We sample the next state, and it may be new, or already in the tree. And the backup discounts the return, with gamma. On the right, the move and the next state are one single node. UCB1 maximizes the win rate of the player to move. And the backup gives each node the reward of its own player: z for one player, one minus z for the other.

Theoretical guarantees of UCT

At each node, UCB1 faces a non-stationary bandit: the returns of a child drift as its own subtree improves.

Theorem (Kocsis & Szepesvári, 2006). For a finite-horizon MDP or game with rewards in $[0, 1]$ and a suitable exploration constant:

  • the bias of the value estimated at the root is $O\!\left(\frac{\ln n}{n}\right)$;
  • the probability of choosing a suboptimal action at the root tends to $0$ (at a polynomial rate).

But the guarantee is asymptotic: there are trees of depth $D$ where UCT needs a number of iterations that is a tower of $D - 1$ nested exponentials (Coquelin & Munos, 2007). In practice: tune $c$, normalize the returns, use good rollouts or a learned evaluation.

Chaque nœud est un bandit non stationnaire. Théorème de Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006) : biais en O(ln n / n) à la racine, probabilité d'erreur qui tend vers 0. Mais convergence potentiellement extrêmement lente dans le pire cas (Coquelin & Munos, 2007).
What does the theory say? Inside the tree, each node is a bandit, but a non-stationary one. The returns of a child change over time, because its own subtree keeps improving. So the classic analysis of UCB1 does not apply directly. [click] Still, Kocsis and Szepesvári proved in 2006 that UCT works, for a finite horizon and rewards between 0 and 1. [click] The bias of the value at the root decreases like log n over n. And the probability of playing a wrong action at the root goes to zero. [click] But this is an asymptotic result. Coquelin and Munos found trees where UCT needs an absurd number of iterations: a tower of exponentials. In practice, we tune c, we normalize the returns, and we use good rollouts or a learned evaluation. We saw a mild version of this slowness on FrozenLake.

Properties and limits

Properties

  • Anytime: stop at any time with a usable answer
  • Asymmetric growth: the budget goes to promising lines
  • Needs only a simulator: no evaluation function, no domain knowledge
  • Easy to parallelize (root, leaf or tree parallelization)
  • The subtree of the played action is reused at the next step

Limits

  • Random rollouts are poor evaluators: sparse rewards (FrozenLake), tactical traps (chess: Ramanujan et al., 2010)
  • Very large branching factors
  • No generalization: identical or similar states are learned separately ⇒ transposition tables, RAVE / AMAF (Gelly & Silver, 2007), learned evaluations
Propriétés : anytime, croissance asymétrique, n'a besoin que d'un simulateur, parallélisable, réutilisation du sous-arbre. Limites : rollouts aléatoires peu informatifs (récompenses rares, pièges tactiques), grands facteurs de branchement, absence de généralisation entre états.
Let's sum up the strengths of MCTS. It is anytime: we can stop it whenever we want. The tree grows asymmetrically, where it matters. It only needs a simulator: no hand-made evaluation function. It is easy to parallelize. And after a real move, we can reuse the subtree of the move we played. [click] Now, the limits. Random rollouts are weak evaluators. With sparse rewards, like in FrozenLake, they give very noisy estimates. In tactical games like chess, a random playout misses the traps. Very large branching factors are also hard. And there is no generalization: identical or similar states are learned separately. Transposition tables, RAVE, or learned evaluations help. These limits are exactly what the modern variants fix.

Modern MCTS: AlphaGo, AlphaZero, MuZero

AlphaGo (Silver et al., 2016): MCTS guided by a policy network and a value network (plus fast rollouts). AlphaGo Zero (2017), AlphaZero (2018): a single network $f_\theta(s) = (\boldsymbol{p}, v)$ gives a prior $P(s, a)$ over the moves and a value $v(s)$.

Selection with PUCT: the prior replaces the uniform exploration of UCB1 $$ a = \arg\max_a \; Q(s, a) + c_{\text{puct}} \, P(s, a) \, \frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1 + N(s, a)} $$

No rollout (AlphaGo Zero, AlphaZero): the new leaf is evaluated by the value network $v(s)$.

Self-play: the network learns to predict MCTS's visit distribution and the game outcome — MCTS acts as a policy improvement operator.

MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020): MCTS in a learned model — no simulator needed.

AlphaGo : MCTS guidé par un réseau de politique et un réseau de valeur (plus des rollouts rapides). AlphaGo Zero/AlphaZero : un seul réseau donne un a priori sur les coups et une valeur. Sélection PUCT, évaluation des feuilles par le réseau de valeur au lieu des rollouts, apprentissage par self-play où MCTS sert d'opérateur d'amélioration de politique. MuZero : MCTS dans un modèle appris.
The modern versions keep the tree, and fix these limits with neural networks. AlphaGo, in 2016, already used a policy network and a value network, plus fast rollouts. In AlphaGo Zero and AlphaZero, a single network gives two things: a prior over the moves, and a value. [click] The prior enters the selection rule, called PUCT. It replaces the uniform exploration bonus of UCB1: promising moves are explored first. [click] And the value network replaces the random rollout: a new leaf is evaluated directly. [click] The network is trained by self-play. It learns to predict the visit counts of MCTS, and the final result of the game. MCTS improves the policy, and the network learns it: MCTS is a policy improvement operator. [click] Finally, MuZero runs MCTS inside a learned model: no simulator is needed anymore.

Take-home messages

  • MCTS = best-first tree search, guided by bandits (UCB1) and evaluated by Monte Carlo simulations
  • Four phases per iteration: selection, expansion, simulation, backpropagation
  • The setting changes the details:
    • stochastic: chance nodes, sampled outcomes, discounted returns
    • two-player zero-sum: one node per position, values seen by each player
  • Consistent (UCT) but slow: the decision converges before the values
  • State of the art: learned priors and values instead of random rollouts (AlphaZero, MuZero)
Messages à retenir : recherche best-first guidée par des bandits et évaluée par Monte Carlo ; quatre phases ; ce qui change selon le cadre ; consistance mais lenteur ; variantes modernes.
To conclude, the key ideas to remember. MCTS is a best-first tree search, guided by bandits, and evaluated by Monte Carlo simulations. Each iteration has the same four phases: selection, expansion, simulation, backpropagation. The skeleton never changes, but the setting changes the details. For stochastic environments: chance nodes, sampled outcomes, and discounted returns. For two-player games: one node per position, and the point of view of each player. It converges, but slowly: the decision is right long before the values are. And the state of the art replaces random rollouts with learned priors and values, like AlphaZero.
