Hierarchical RL

Introduction

Human analogy: planning a trip vs. walking to the station

Let's start with something we do every day, without even thinking. Humans are very good at breaking down a complex task into smaller subtasks. We solve them one by one, and together they solve the original problem. And these subtasks live at different levels of abstraction. Look at this picture: a trip from your home to a hotel, in a distant city. At the highest level, you decide which flight to take, and which airport to land at. Then come mid-level decisions: from the airport, do you take the train or the bus? Then lower-level decisions, like taking a taxi for the last part of the trip. And at the very bottom, your steps and your body movements. You do them without even thinking about them. Now, a classical RL agent has none of this structure. It would plan the whole trip one footstep at a time. Over millions of time steps, that's hopeless. Structuring decisions on several levels: that's exactly what hierarchical reinforcement learning tries to capture.

Hierarchical RL

Introduction

Here is the same idea, for a robot. At the top, the high level. It makes abstract decisions, like "go to the kitchen". It doesn't care about motors or wheels. At the bottom, the low level. It deals with the details, like "avoid this obstacle" on the way. It doesn't need to know why the robot goes to the kitchen. Each level solves a simpler problem. The high level thinks in rooms, over a long time. The low level thinks in small movements, over a short time. That's the core idea of a hierarchy: different levels, different time scales.

Hierarchical RL

What and Why?

  • Decompose complex tasks into simpler subtasks
  • Learn policies at multiple levels of abstraction
  • Motivation: scalability, efficiency, transferability
So, what is hierarchical RL? Three ideas on this slide. First, we decompose a complex task into simpler subtasks. Many sequential decision problems have some structure, and we exploit it. Second, we learn policies at multiple levels of abstraction. A high-level policy chooses which subtask to do. Low-level policies solve each subtask, with primitive actions. And why do we do this? Mainly for three reasons. Scalability: we can solve longer and harder tasks. Efficiency: we need fewer samples to learn. And transferability: a skill learned for one task can be reused in another one. We'll come back to these advantages in a minute.

Hierarchical RL

Applications

Some problems have natural hierarchical structure

  • Navigation tasks: route planning + motor control
  • Multi-agent systems: hierarchical team organization
  • Robotics: task planning + low-level control
  • Game playing: strategy + tactical execution
La HRL est utilisée en robotique, jeux, NLP, véhicules autonomes, etc.
So, where is it useful? Some problems have a natural hierarchical structure. And when the structure is clear, it's easy to exploit. Navigation: plan the route at a high level, and control the motors at a low level. Multi-agent systems: like in a company, agents can be organized in teams, with leaders and members. Robotics: plan the task, for example "pick up the cup, then put it in the sink". Then a low-level controller moves the arm. And games: think of a strategy game. The player chooses a global strategy, and then executes it with many tactical actions. On other problems, the structure is less obvious. And then, finding the right hierarchy is much harder.

Hierarchical RL

Advantages

  • Improved sample efficiency
  • Better exploration
  • Transfer and reuse of subpolicies
  • Scalability to complex tasks
So, what do we gain with a hierarchy? First, sample efficiency. The high level makes far fewer decisions per episode. So its effective horizon is much shorter, and credit assignment becomes easier. And the subpolicies are reused again and again. Second, better exploration. With random primitive actions, the agent mostly stays near its starting point. With random subgoals, it travels far, to really new places. Third, transfer. A skill like "go through a door" can be reused in many tasks. And finally, scalability: we can tackle much larger problems. Look at this image: StarCraft, a real-time strategy game. A huge number of possible actions, and very long games. A hierarchy of strategy, tactics and unit control is a natural way to handle it.

Hierarchical RL

Challenges

  • Find subgoals
  • Find a meta-policy over these subgoals
  • Find subpolicies for these subgoals
But there is a catch. To build a hierarchy, we have to answer three questions. First, what are the good subgoals? For example, in a building, the doors are natural subgoals. Second, how do we choose between these subgoals? That's the meta-policy: a policy over subgoals. Third, how do we reach each subgoal? That's the job of the subpolicies. Each of these three problems is hard. And ideally, we'd like to learn all three from experience.

The options framework

Sutton, R. S., Precup, D., & Singh, S. (1999). Between MDPs and semi-MDPs: A framework for temporal abstraction in reinforcement learning. Artificial Intelligence, 112(1-2), 181-211.

So, how do we describe a hierarchy formally? We need a framework where actions can last for different durations. The most famous one is the options framework. It was introduced by Sutton, Precup and Singh, in 1999. Yes, the same Sutton as in the famous RL textbook. The idea: add temporally extended actions, called options, to the standard MDP. An option is a subpolicy, with its own start and stop conditions. So the agent can act at different time scales.

The options framework

Motivation: Temporal Abstraction in RL

  • Standard MDPs only support primitive actions at each timestep.
  • Need: a way to represent and learn temporally extended behaviors.
  • Goal: extend RL framework minimally to include such temporal abstractions.

👉 Solution: Extend standard MDPs to support temporally extended actions called options

Why do we need it? In a standard MDP, the agent only has primitive actions. One action, one time step. Move one cell, or apply one torque. But we want to represent temporally extended behaviors. Things like "walk to the door", which take many time steps. The goal is to add these temporal abstractions to the RL framework. And to do it in a minimal way, so that we can keep our usual tools, like Q-learning. The solution: extend the MDP with temporally extended actions, called options.

The options framework

What is an Option?

  • Some states are designated as subgoals (e.g., doorways)
  • An option (macro-action) is a subpolicy that drives the agent towards a subgoal
  • Where an option is available, the agent can choose it instead of a primitive action
  • Executing an option means following its subpolicy until it terminates (e.g., at the subgoal)
The idea of options is simple. Some states are chosen as subgoals. For example, the doorways between rooms. An option, or macro-action, is a subpolicy that drives the agent to one of these subgoals. Look at the figure. On the left, primitive actions: up, down, left, right. One cell at a time. On the right, an option: one single decision takes the agent all the way to the gray cell. Where an option is available, the agent can choose it, instead of a primitive action. Then it follows the subpolicy until the option terminates, usually at the subgoal. Note: an option is not a fixed sequence of actions. The subpolicy looks at the current state at each step, so it can react to what happens.

The options framework

What is an Option?

An option $\omega$ is defined by a triple: $$\omega = \langle I_{\omega}, \pi_{\omega}, \beta_{\omega} \rangle$$

$I_{\omega}$ The initiation set $I_{\omega} \subseteq \mathcal{S}$: the states where the option can be chosen.
$\pi_{\omega}(a|s)$ The subpolicy $\pi_{\omega} : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow [0, 1]$ controls which primitive actions are taken while the option runs (here a stochastic policy).
$\beta_{\omega}(s)$ The termination condition $\beta_{\omega} : \mathcal{S} \rightarrow [0, 1]$ gives the probability that $\omega$ terminates in $s$.
More formally, an option, omega, is defined by three elements. First, the initiation set, I omega. It's the set of states where the option can be started. For example, all the cells of one room. Second, the subpolicy, pi omega. It chooses the primitive actions while the option is running. Here, it's a stochastic policy: a probability for each action, in each state. Third, the termination condition, beta omega. For each state, it gives the probability that the option stops there. For example, beta is one at the doorway, and zero everywhere else in the room. So: where to start, how to act, and when to stop.

The options framework


The set of all options is denoted by $\Omega$.

Now, an agent usually has several options. For example, one option for each doorway. We call the set of all options capital omega. And note: a primitive action can be seen as a special option. An option that lasts exactly one step. So the agent can mix options and primitive actions in the same framework.

The options framework

There are two kinds of policies in the options framework:

  • The meta-policy over options $\pi_{\Omega}(\omega|s)$, which, each time the current option terminates, selects the next option to execute among those whose initiation set contains the current state.
  • The subpolicy of each option $\pi_{\omega}(a|s)$, which defines the behavior while the option is being executed.
So, in the options framework, we have two kinds of policies. At the top, the meta-policy, pi capital omega. It's a policy over options. Each time the current option terminates, it selects the next option to run. And it can only choose options whose initiation set contains the current state. At the bottom, the subpolicy of each option, pi omega. It chooses the primitive actions while the option is running. So the meta-policy decides what to do. And the subpolicy decides how to do it.

The options framework


The subpolicies $\pi_{\omega}$ act like macros: they take the agent from a state of $I_{\omega}$ to a state where the option terminates (according to $\beta_{\omega}$).

You can see the subpolicies as macros. Like a macro in a text editor: one command, and many steps are executed. A subpolicy starts in a state of its initiation set. And it brings the agent to a state where the option terminates, according to beta. For the meta-policy, this whole trip is one single decision. So the top level makes far fewer decisions, and each one is more meaningful.

The options framework

"Temporal abstraction mix actions of different granularities, short and long, primitive actions and subpolicies. They allow traveling from $I$ to $\beta$ without additional learning, using a previously provided or learned subpolicy." (Plaat, 2022)

Here is a nice summary, from Aske Plaat's book on deep reinforcement learning. Temporal abstraction mixes actions of different granularities. Short ones and long ones. Primitive actions, and subpolicies. A subpolicy lets the agent go from I to beta, that is, from a start state to a stop state. And this needs no additional learning. It simply reuses a subpolicy that was given, or learned before.

The rooms example

  • A gridworld environment with stochastic cell-to-cell actions and room-to-room hallway options.
  • Two of the hallway options are suggested by the arrows labeled o1 and o2.
  • The labels G1 and G2 indicate two locations used as goals in the experiments of the paper.
Here is the classic example from the options paper: the four rooms. It's a gridworld: four rooms, connected by four hallways. The agent has four primitive actions: up, down, left, right. And they are stochastic: they fail one third of the time. The hallways are bottleneck states: to go from one room to another, you must go through them. So they are natural subgoals. So we define eight hallway options: in each room, one option for each of its two hallways. Look at the bottom-left room. Option o1 takes the agent from anywhere in this room to the hallway leading up, to the top-left room. Option o2 takes it to the other hallway, the one leading to the bottom-right room. G1 and G2 are two goal locations, used in the experiments of the paper. With these options, the agent can plan room by room, instead of cell by cell. And planning and learning become much faster.

Finding subgoals and learning subpolicies

In the original options framework, these elements are given (designed by hand):

  • Subgoals
  • Subpolicies (the options)

Only the meta-policy is learned, with standard RL methods (e.g., SMDP Q-learning).

But, in practice, subgoals and subpolicies often need to be learned too.

In the original options framework, the structure of the problem is given. The subgoals and the subpolicies are designed by hand. Only the meta-policy is learned. And for that, we can use standard RL methods, like SMDP Q-learning. SMDP means semi-Markov decision process. It's just Q-learning, where an option is treated like an action that lasts several time steps. The only change is in the update. We use the rewards collected during the option, and we discount by gamma to the power k, where k is its duration. But in real applications, nobody gives us the subgoals and the subpolicies. So they must be learned too.

Finding subgoals and learning subpolicies

👉 We need algorithms that can find subgoals and learn subpolicies

So we need algorithms that can find subgoals and learn subpolicies, automatically. Ideally, from experience only, with no hand-made structure. Let's look at some of them.

Some algorithms

For large or continuous state spaces

  • Feudal Networks
  • Option-Critic
  • HIRO
  • Hierarchical Actor-Critic (HAC)
Here are four important hierarchical RL algorithms. They all use deep neural networks. So they work in large or continuous state spaces, like images or robot sensors. Feudal Networks and Option-Critic were tested with discrete actions, for example on Atari games. HIRO and HAC also handle continuous actions, for robot control. I'll give a very short overview of each one. And I'll spend a bit more time on Hierarchical Actor-Critic. It shows clearly how a multi-level hierarchy of goal-conditioned policies can be learned.

Feudal Networks

Key Idea:

  • Inspired by Feudal RL (Dayan & Hinton, 1992): a Manager–Worker hierarchy.
  • Manager sets abstract goals in a latent space.
  • Worker executes primitive actions conditioned on these goals.

Tested on (Vezhnevets et al., 2017): Atari games, DeepMind Lab (3D)

Achievements:

  • Learned effective temporal abstractions (macro-actions).
  • Showed faster learning and better exploration on hard sparse-reward environments (e.g., Montezuma's Revenge) compared to a flat deep RL baseline (LSTM agent trained with A3C).
  • Demonstrated that latent goal vectors can guide behavior without explicit subgoal supervision.
Let's start with Feudal Networks. The idea comes from Feudal RL, by Dayan and Hinton, in 1992. Like in the Middle Ages: a lord gives orders to a vassal. Here, a Manager gives orders to a Worker. The Manager works at a slow time scale. It sets abstract goals, as directions in a learned latent space. The Worker acts at every time step. It chooses primitive actions to follow the Manager's current goal. It gets an intrinsic reward when the state moves in the direction asked by the Manager. In 2017, DeepMind tested this on Atari games, and on DeepMind Lab, a 3D environment. The agent learned useful temporal abstractions. It learned faster than a flat baseline: an LSTM agent, trained with A3C. Especially on hard games with sparse rewards, like Montezuma's Revenge. And no one told it what the subgoals should be.

Feudal Networks

Position Today:

  • Popularized the goal-setting HRL paradigm in deep RL.
  • However: rarely used in practice now
    • Instability in training.
    • Limited scalability to very complex tasks.
  • Key historical impact: introduced goals in a learned latent space, with Manager and Worker trained separately (no gradient between levels), still influential in modern HRL research.

References:

  • Dayan, P., & Hinton, G. E. (1992). Feudal reinforcement learning. Advances in neural information processing systems, 5.
  • Vezhnevets, A. S., Osindero, S., Schaul, T., Heess, N., Jaderberg, M., Silver, D., & Kavukcuoglu, K. (2017). FeUdal networks for hierarchical reinforcement learning. In International Conference on Machine Learning (pp. 3540-3549). PMLR.
So, where does it stand today? Feudal Networks popularized the goal-setting paradigm in deep hierarchical RL. One level sets goals, and the level below tries to reach them. But it's rarely used in practice now. Training is unstable. And it doesn't scale well to very complex tasks. Its main historical impact: goals in a learned latent space. And the Manager and the Worker are trained separately. No gradient flows from the Worker to the Manager. Each level has its own learning signal. This idea is still influential in modern hierarchical RL. The references are on the slide: Dayan and Hinton, 1992, and Vezhnevets and colleagues, 2017.

Option-Critic

Key Idea:

  • A policy gradient algorithm.
  • Learn intra-option policies, termination functions, and a policy over options.

Tested on:

  • Four-room navigation domain
  • Atari 2600 (Arcade Learning Environment)
  • Pinball domain (continuous state space, discrete actions)

Achievements:

  • First fully differentiable framework for learning options.
  • Demonstrated that options can emerge naturally without hand-crafted subgoals.
  • Faster recovery than flat RL baselines when the goal changes (four-room); outperformed DQN on 3 of the 4 Atari games tested.
Next, Option-Critic, by Bacon, Harb and Precup, in 2017. Doina Precup is also one of the authors of the options framework. It's a policy gradient method. The key idea: learn everything at once. The intra-option policies, the termination functions, and the policy over options. All with policy gradients, end to end. No need to define subgoals by hand. It was tested on the four-rooms domain, on Atari games, and on Pinball. Pinball has a continuous state space, with discrete actions. The results? It was the first fully differentiable framework to learn options. It showed that options can emerge naturally, with no hand-crafted subgoals. In the four rooms, when the goal moves, it recovers faster than flat agents. And it beat DQN on three of the four Atari games they tested.

Option-Critic

Position Today:

  • A foundational baseline for HRL; many later works extend it (e.g., option discovery, regularization, diversity constraints).
  • Still conceptually influential, though modern state-of-the-art often favors skill discovery methods (e.g., DIAYN, VALOR) or hierarchical RL with pretrained skills.

References:

  • Bacon, P. L., Harb, J., & Precup, D. (2017, February). The option-critic architecture. In Proceedings of the AAAI conference on artificial intelligence (Vol. 31, No. 1).
Today, Option-Critic is a foundational baseline for hierarchical RL. Many later works extend it. For example, to discover better options, to regularize them, or to make them more diverse. Why regularize? A known problem: without constraints, the options tend to terminate at every step, or one option does everything. And then, there is no real hierarchy anymore. It's still very influential, as an idea. But modern methods often prefer skill discovery, like DIAYN or VALOR. These methods first learn a set of diverse skills, without any reward. Or they use hierarchical RL with pretrained skills.

HIRO

Key Idea:

  • Off-policy correction: past high-level goals are relabeled with the goal that best explains the low-level actions actually taken, under the current low-level policy → both levels can be trained off-policy despite the changing low level → improves sample efficiency.

Tested on:

  • MuJoCo Ant tasks (Ant Gather, Ant Maze, Ant Push, Ant Fall).

Achievements:

  • Demonstrated significant gains in sample efficiency vs. previous HRL methods (a flat agent makes almost no progress on these tasks).
  • Learned complex long-horizon behaviors (e.g., pushing a block out of the way to reach the goal).
Next, HIRO, by Nachum and colleagues, at Google, in 2018. HIRO stands for HIerarchical Reinforcement learning with Off-policy correction. Two levels: a high-level policy proposes goals, and a low-level policy tries to reach them. The challenge: we want to train both levels off-policy, with a replay buffer, to be sample-efficient. But the low-level policy keeps changing. So an old high-level transition is no longer valid: the same goal would now produce different actions. HIRO's trick: the off-policy correction. It relabels the old goals. Each one is replaced by the goal that best explains the low-level actions actually taken, under the current low-level policy. It was tested on MuJoCo Ant tasks: Ant Gather, Ant Maze, Ant Push and Ant Fall. It was much more sample-efficient than previous hierarchical methods. A flat agent makes almost no progress on these tasks. And HIRO learned complex long-horizon behaviors, like pushing a block out of the way to reach the goal.

HIRO

Position Today:

  • Considered a foundational baseline for HRL.
  • Later methods (e.g. HRAC, HIGL, HiPPO) refine stability, exploration, or scalability.
  • Still cited as one of the most influential HRL approaches for long-horizon continuous control.

References:

  • Nachum, O., Gu, S. S., Lee, H., & Levine, S. (2018). Data-efficient hierarchical reinforcement learning. Advances in neural information processing systems, 31.
  • Related work: Nachum, O., Gu, S., Lee, H., & Levine, S. (2018). Near-optimal representation learning for hierarchical reinforcement learning. arXiv preprint arXiv:1810.01257.
Today, HIRO is considered a foundational baseline for hierarchical RL. Later methods refine it. For example HRAC, HIGL, or HiPPO. They improve its stability, its exploration, or its scalability. It's still cited as one of the most influential approaches for long-horizon continuous control. The main paper is on the slide: "Data-efficient hierarchical reinforcement learning", from 2018. The same authors also published a related paper, on how to learn a good representation for the goals.

Hierarchical Actor-Critic

Key Idea:

  • Train multiple policy levels, each setting goals for the level below.
  • Use hindsight at every level: hindsight action transitions (the proposed subgoal is replaced by the state actually reached) to cope with non-stationarity, and Hindsight Experience Replay to cope with sparse rewards.

Tested on:

  • MuJoCo

Achievements:

  • Can learn more than two hierarchical levels.
  • Jointly learns multiple levels of policies in parallel (subproblems can be solved simultaneously).
Finally, Hierarchical Actor-Critic, or HAC, by Levy and colleagues. The key idea: train several levels of policies. Each level sets goals for the level below. The lowest level outputs the actual actions. The difficulty: all levels learn at the same time. So, for a high level, the level below is non-stationary: its behavior keeps changing. HAC solves this with hindsight, at every level. First, hindsight action transitions. When a high level proposed a subgoal, we replace it by the state that was actually reached. So, from the high level's point of view, the level below looks like an optimal and stable policy. Second, Hindsight Experience Replay, against sparse rewards. A failed attempt becomes a success, for the goal that was actually reached. It was tested on MuJoCo tasks. And it can learn more than two levels, all in parallel.

Hierarchical Actor-Critic

Position Today:

  • Pioneering work: first to learn 3-level hierarchies in parallel in tasks with continuous state and action spaces.
  • Inspired many follow-ups in hierarchical goal-conditioned RL.

References:

  • Levy, A., Konidaris, G., Platt, R., & Saenko, K. (2017). Learning multi-level hierarchies with hindsight. arXiv preprint arXiv:1712.00948
HAC is a pioneering work. It was the first method to learn three-level hierarchies in parallel, in tasks with continuous states and actions. And it inspired many follow-ups in hierarchical goal-conditioned RL. Goal-conditioned means that the policy takes the state as input, and also the goal to reach. The reference is on the slide: Levy, Konidaris, Platt and Saenko. It was first posted on arXiv in 2017, and published at ICLR in 2019.

Hierarchical Actor-Critic

Here is how it works, on a concrete task. An ant robot must go through four rooms, to reach the yellow box. The yellow box is the task goal. On the left, the three levels. Each policy takes the current state, the joint angles theta and their velocities theta dot, plus a goal. The top level, pi two, gets the yellow goal, and outputs a subgoal: the green box. The middle level, pi one, gets the green goal, and outputs a closer subgoal: the magenta box. And the bottom level, pi zero, gets the magenta goal, and outputs the joint torques. So each level only has to solve a short-horizon problem: reach a goal that is close. And together, they solve a long task.
