Overview

So, to begin with, here is the big picture: how a modern large language model is trained. There are several stages, from left to right. First, pre-training, then instruction fine-tuning. These two stages use self-supervised and supervised learning. Then come preference fine-tuning, and reasoning fine-tuning. Look at the black area on the right: these two stages use reinforcement learning. Above each model, you can see an example of what it answers. Let's go through these stages, one by one.

Overview

The model is first trained using self-supervised learning on a massive amount of text data.

It all starts with a randomly initialized model. Say "Hello!" to it, and it answers random garbage: "the the bip bop boop". So the model is first trained with self-supervised learning, on a massive amount of text. The task is simple: predict the next token. No labels are needed: the text itself is the supervision. This is pre-training, and it's by far the most expensive stage in compute.

Overview

This produces a sophisticated auto-completer that cannot easily interact with humans.

This produces a base model. It's very good at completing text. Look at the example: "It's raining cats and", and the model continues: "dogs. I should get an umbrella." But it's essentially a sophisticated auto-completer. It cannot reliably answer questions, or follow instructions. And it cannot hold a natural conversation. Ask it a question, and it may just continue with more questions.

Overview

To make the model more useful we apply instruction fine-tuning or Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

To make the model more useful, we fine-tune it with supervised learning. This stage is called instruction fine-tuning, or supervised fine-tuning, SFT. We use a dataset of instructions, paired with high-quality answers written by humans. And we train the model to imitate these answers. It's the same next-token prediction as before, but on carefully chosen data. The model learns the format of a dialogue: a question, then a helpful answer.

Overview

Now the model can follow instructions, answer questions, and chat more naturally.
But it may still generate outputs that are misaligned with human preferences.

The result is an instruction-tuned model. Now it can follow instructions, answer questions, and chat more naturally. Ask it "What ice cream should I get?", and it gives a real answer: "Vanilla is the best flavor in the world." But it may still generate outputs that are misaligned with human preferences. Answers that are correct, but not the ones people prefer. Or answers that are unhelpful, wrong, or even harmful. And SFT alone can't easily fix this, as we'll see.

Overview

A second stage of fine-tuning is applied. Historically, OpenAI used PPO within the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (or RLHF) framework to achieve this.

To fix this, a second stage of fine-tuning is applied: preference fine-tuning. And this is the first stage where reinforcement learning comes in. Look at the example: same question, but now the answer is "Chocolate is the best flavor". Why chocolate? Because, in our little example, that's what people prefer. The model now gives the answers that humans like best. Historically, OpenAI did this with PPO, Proximal Policy Optimization. Inside a framework called Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, or RLHF.

Preference-based fine-tuning

Motivations

Why do we need a second stage of fine-tuning?

  • Supervised fine-tuning alone cannot guarantee outputs that are fully aligned with human preferences.
  • Collecting larger, high-quality human-labeled datasets is too expensive or impractical.

Preference-based fine-tuning only asks humans to compare model outputs (which answer is better?) instead of writing ideal answers → much cheaper.

So, why do we need this second stage? Mainly for two reasons. First, as I said, supervised fine-tuning alone can't guarantee outputs that are fully aligned with human preferences. Second, the cost of data. To go further with SFT, we would need larger datasets of high-quality answers, written by humans. That's too expensive, or just impractical. Preference fine-tuning uses another kind of human feedback. The annotator doesn't write the ideal answer. They just compare a few answers generated by the model, and say which one is better. This is much cheaper, and faster. And it also works when people can recognize a good answer more easily than they can write one. Think of a poem, or a good summary: easy to judge, hard to write.

Preference-based fine-tuning

With RLHF/PPO

So, let's see how preference fine-tuning works with RLHF and PPO. But first, we need to take a step back, and generalize the RL framework a little bit. Because, for a chatbot, there is no reward function given by the environment.

Learn from feedback

A generalization of the RL framework

  • RL agents need feedback to learn preferred behaviors
  • Usually provided by a reward function (maps states/actions to scalar values)
  • Explicit reward functions can be hard to design in real-world tasks
  • Alternative feedback sources (e.g., human preferences, demonstrations) can guide learning
  • This generalizes RL to "learning from feedback"
To learn, an RL agent needs feedback: some signal that tells it which behaviors are preferred. Usually, this feedback is a reward function. It maps states and actions to a scalar value. But in many real-world tasks, an explicit reward function is very hard to design. What is the reward for a "good" answer of a chatbot? So we can use other kinds of feedback to guide learning. For example, human preferences, or demonstrations. Look at the diagram. The usual loop: the agent acts on the environment, and receives observations. And in the middle, a new box: the feedback provider. It watches the observations and the actions, and it sends feedback to the agent. This generalizes RL into "learning from feedback".

Reward Model

A quick introduction

  • When a reward function is unavailable, we may learn a reward model from data (e.g., expert demonstrations, human preferences).
  • Use the reward model’s output as reward signals for RL algorithms.
  • Learning it from expert demonstrations is known as Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL).
  • IRL will be covered in more detail next week.
So, what can we do when no reward function is available? We can learn one. We train a reward model from data. Then, we use its output as the reward signal for a standard RL algorithm. What kind of data? When the data are expert demonstrations, it's called Inverse Reinforcement Learning, or IRL. We infer the reward that the expert seems to optimize. We'll come back to IRL in more detail later in the course. In the next slides, the reward model is learned from another kind of data: human preferences.

PbRL: Preference-based RL

Key Idea:

  • Instead of scalar rewards, use preferences over trajectory segments as feedback.
  • Train a reward model to predict which segment is preferred.
  • Use the learned reward model to provide reward signals for RL algorithms.
  • Enables learning from comparative feedback rather than absolute rewards.

👉 When the environment does not provide a reward function, but a preference feedback, we do Preference-based RL (PbRL).

Here is one example of feedback that is not a scalar reward: preferences. We show two trajectory segments to someone, and we ask: which one do you prefer? Then we train a reward model to predict these preferences. The idea: the preferred segment should get a higher total reward. Technically, the probability of preferring A over B is a sigmoid of the difference of their rewards. And the learned reward model provides the reward signal for the RL algorithm. So we learn from comparative feedback, not from absolute rewards. It's often easier for a person to say "A is better than B" than to give a score, like 7 out of 10. So, when the environment gives preference feedback instead of a reward, we call it Preference-based RL, or PbRL.

RLHF

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

👉 When this preference feedback is provided by a human during training, we do Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

Now, what if this preference feedback comes from a human, during training? Then we call it Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, or RLHF. Look at the diagram. At the bottom, the usual RL loop: the RL algorithm sends actions to the environment, and gets observations. But the reward does not come from the environment. It comes from a reward predictor, in purple. The reward predictor is trained with human feedback, on the right. And it gives a predicted reward to the RL algorithm. These three processes run in parallel, asynchronously. The agent acts, the human compares clips, and the reward predictor is updated.

RLHF

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

  • RLHF introduced by OpenAI and DeepMind (2017) for simulated robots and Atari games.
  • First, agent acts randomly; human compares pairs of video clips and selects the one closer to the objective (e.g., backflip).
  • Algorithm learns a reward function that matches human preferences.
  • Uses RL to optimize agent behavior toward the learned goal.
  • Continually requests human feedback on uncertain trajectory pairs to refine the reward model.
  • High feedback efficiency: backflip task required less than 1000 bits of human feedback.
RLHF was introduced in 2017, by OpenAI and DeepMind. At first, it was not for language at all: it was for simulated robots and Atari games. Here is how it works. In the beginning, the agent acts randomly. Periodically, a human watches two short video clips of its behavior, like the two on the slide. And they click: "left is better", or "right is better". The question is: which clip is closer to the objective? Here, the objective is to do a backflip. The algorithm builds a model of the goal: the reward function that best explains the human's choices. Then it uses RL to reach that goal. As it improves, it asks for feedback on the pairs where it is most uncertain. It is very efficient in human feedback: the backflip needed about 900 bits of feedback. One bit is roughly one comparison: left or right.

RLHF

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

And here is the result: a backflip. It took less than an hour of a human evaluator's time. And there was no explicit reward function at all. Try to write a reward function for a nice backflip by hand. It's really hard! Here, the human just had to recognize good attempts. To be fair, it's not cheap in interactions with the environment. In the background, the agent collected about 70 hours of simulated experience.

RLHF

Application to LLMs

🤨
  • RLHF was first developed for training robots and Atari agents.
  • Quickly adapted for large language models (LLMs).
  • ChatGPT users regularly see feedback requests.
  • User feedback is leveraged to further align LLMs via RLHF.
OK, so RLHF was first developed for robots and Atari agents. But it was quickly adapted to large language models. You have probably seen this in ChatGPT: "Which response do you prefer?" That's exactly a preference comparison. And this free work from users can then be used to further align the model. Now, look closely at the two responses. They are exactly the same! Hence the little emoji on the slide. Not every comparison is very informative.

RLHF

Application to LLMs

In 2020, OpenAI used RLHF to train models to summarize text. The figure shows the three steps of RLHF. Step one, collect human feedback. We take a Reddit post, and several policies generate summaries. A human compares two of them, and says which one is better: "j is better than k". Step two, train a reward model. It gives a score r to each summary. The loss pushes the score of the preferred summary above the other one. We maximize the log of a sigmoid of r j minus r k. Step three, train the policy with PPO. For a new post, the policy writes a summary, and the reward model scores it. PPO uses this score as the reward to update the policy. And a KL penalty keeps the policy close to the initial model, to avoid reward hacking.

RLHF

Application to LLMs

And in 2022, OpenAI used it to train InstructGPT. A GPT-3 model, fine-tuned to follow instructions with human feedback. It's the direct predecessor of ChatGPT. Again, three steps. Step one: for a prompt, like "explain the moon landing to a 6 year old", a labeler writes the desired answer. GPT-3 is fine-tuned on these answers: that's SFT. Step two: the model generates several answers, and a labeler ranks them, from best to worst. These rankings train the reward model. Step three: PPO optimizes the policy against the reward model. So, this is the full pipeline of the overview: SFT first, then preference fine-tuning with RL.

RLHF

Application to LLMs

Did it work? Yes: human evaluators clearly preferred InstructGPT to GPT-3. On this plot, the y-axis is the win rate against the 175-billion-parameter SFT model. That is, how often each model's answers were preferred to that model's answers. The x-axis is the model size. At the bottom, the two GPT-3 curves, in blue: even with a good prompt, their win rate stays low. In green, the SFT models. At the top, the two RLHF models: PPO, and PPO-ptx, the actual InstructGPT. PPO-ptx also mixes some pretraining gradients into the PPO updates. It limits regressions on standard NLP benchmarks. And here is the key result. The 1.3 billion InstructGPT is preferred to the 175 billion GPT-3. A model more than 100 times smaller!

RLHF

Advantages and drawbacks

Advantage:

  • Compared to offline preference fine-tuning methods such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), the Reward Model can score new outputs generated by the policy during training → well-tuned RLHF often performs better with the same human feedback.

Drawbacks:

  • Training can be unstable; the Reward Model may be inaccurate, leading to misaligned or unintended behaviors.
  • The PPO policy may exploit Reward Model weakness (reward hacking).
So, why use RL here, and not something simpler? RLHF has one big advantage. Compare it with offline methods, like Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO. DPO learns only from a fixed dataset of compared answers. In RLHF, the learned reward model can score new answers, generated by the policy during training. So the same human feedback is exploited further. And well-tuned RLHF with PPO often performs better than DPO. But it also has drawbacks. Training can be unstable, and the reward model may be inaccurate. Then the model learns misaligned or unintended behaviors. Worse, the policy may exploit the weaknesses of the reward model. That's called reward hacking. For example, reward models often prefer longer answers, so the model may learn to be verbose.

Reasoning-based fine-tuning

With RLVR/GRPO

So, let's see how reasoning fine-tuning works, with RLVR and GRPO. And here, good news: we don't even need humans to give the reward.

Reasoning-based fine-tuning

Context

  • Many reasoning tasks (math, coding, logic) have answers that can be automatically verified (e.g., final answer check, unit tests).
  • Unlike creative tasks, no human feedback is needed for evaluation.
  • Enables automatic, scalable assessment of LLM outputs.
  • DeepSeek-R1 adds a fine-tuning stage dedicated to reasoning, using RL with verifiable rewards.
There is a special class of problems called reasoning tasks. Math, programming, logic, and so on. Unlike open-ended or creative tasks, many reasoning tasks have an answer that can be checked automatically. For a math problem, we compare the final answer with the reference answer. For code, we run unit tests. So we can evaluate the LLM's answers automatically, without human feedback. And this is scalable: millions of problems, and no annotators. DeepSeek-R1 exploits this, with a fine-tuning stage dedicated to reasoning, using RL with verifiable rewards. A small warning: the overview diagram is a simplification. In practice, the order of the stages varies. DeepSeek-R1 alternates several SFT and RL stages. Its first RL stage is for reasoning, and its last one also aligns the model with human preferences.

RLVR

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards

So here, RL is used to fine-tune the LLM, with a reward that is computed automatically. The reward simply says whether the final answer is correct. Look at the example at the bottom. Natalia sold 48 clips in April, and half as many in May: how many in total? The model reasons step by step, and ends with 72. The environment checks it: 72 is correct, so the reward R equals one. The reasoning task fits well in the RL framework. The agent is the LLM: its policy outputs a probability for every token of the vocabulary. Each action is one token. The episode ends when the model outputs the end token. The state is the current context: the prompt, plus the tokens generated so far. And the reward comes only at the end: one if the answer is correct, zero otherwise. Look at the box on the right: all the intermediate rewards are zero.

Reasoning-based fine-tuning

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

The RL algorithm used in DeepSeek-R1 is called Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO. It was introduced about one year earlier, in the DeepSeekMath paper. And it's very close to PPO, with two differences. On the left, the usual loop: the LLM outputs a distribution over tokens, samples one, and gets a reward. On the right, how the advantage A t is computed. At the top, PPO. It uses a critic, the value network V phi, in red, to estimate the advantage. In LLMs, this critic is usually as large as the policy itself: it costs a lot of memory and compute. At the bottom, GRPO. First difference: no critic. Instead, for each prompt, we generate a group of answers, here four of them. And the advantage is computed by comparing each answer to the others in the group. Second difference: the KL penalty, which keeps the policy close to the reference model. It's added directly to the loss, instead of being subtracted from the reward, like in RLHF with PPO.

Reasoning-based fine-tuning

GRPO

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t) \cdot \bigl(G_t - b(s_t)\bigr) $$
Let's see where the GRPO advantage comes from. Start with an update rule you already know: REINFORCE with a baseline. We increase the log-probability of each action a t, in state s t. And we weight it by G t minus b of s t. G t is the return from time t, and b is a baseline. If the return is better than the baseline, the action becomes more likely. If it's worse, it becomes less likely. The baseline doesn't change the expected gradient, but it reduces the variance. In actor-critic methods, and in PPO, this baseline is the value function, learned by the critic.

Reasoning-based fine-tuning

GRPO

Now, how does GRPO get its baseline, without a critic? Look at the top row. The prompt is the Natalia problem. The LLM generates one answer, token by token: "To", "find", "out", and so on. Each token is an action, and each circle is a state. The reward comes only at the very end. Now, the trick: for the same prompt, we sample several answers. Here, a group of four. Since the policy is stochastic, the four answers are different. And the environment gives each of them a reward: r g one, to r g four.

Reasoning-based fine-tuning

GRPO

And here is the GRPO baseline. B is simply the mean reward of the group. The intuition: B tells us how hard this prompt is for the current model. If an answer does better than the group average, we push it up. If it does worse, we push it down. No critic network to train: we just sample more answers, and we compare them. In other words, the other answers of the group play the role of the critic.

Reasoning-based fine-tuning

GRPO

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t) \cdot \bigl(G_t - b(s_t)\bigr) $$ Becomes... $$ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t) \cdot (R - B) $$ $$ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t) \cdot A_t $$ $$ A_t = \frac{R - \mathrm{mean}(r_{g_1}, \dots, r_{g_G})}{\mathrm{std}(r_{g_1}, \dots, r_{g_G})} $$

So, let's update our formula. First, the reward is given only at the end, and we don't discount. So the return G t is the same for every token: it's the final reward, capital R. And the baseline b becomes the group mean, capital B. So we get R minus B. Then GRPO also divides by the standard deviation of the group rewards. This gives the advantage A t: the reward minus the group mean, over the group standard deviation. This normalization keeps the gradient scale stable, from one prompt to another. A quick example. Four answers, with rewards one, zero, zero, one. The mean is one half. So the correct answers get a positive advantage, and the wrong ones a negative advantage, of the same size. And if all four answers are correct, all advantages are zero: this prompt teaches nothing.

Reasoning-based fine-tuning

GRPO

$$ \begin{align} L^{PPO} &= \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \min \Bigl( \rho_t(\theta) \cdot A_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \cdot A_t \Bigr) \\[6pt] \rho_t(\theta) &= \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)} \\[6pt] L^{GRPO} &= L^{PPO} - \beta \, D_{KL}\!\left[\pi_{\theta} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\right] \end{align} $$

Finally, GRPO plugs this advantage into the PPO objective. You know this one: L PPO. Rho t is the probability ratio between the new policy and the old one, for action a t. We multiply it by the advantage, and we clip the ratio between one minus epsilon and one plus epsilon. And we take the minimum of the two terms. So one update cannot move the policy too far from the old one. Then, GRPO subtracts a KL divergence term, weighted by beta. It measures how far the policy pi theta is from the reference model pi ref, usually the SFT model. It prevents the model from drifting too far, and from forgetting what it learned before. So, to sum up: GRPO is PPO without a critic, with a group-based advantage, and a KL penalty in the loss.

DeepSeek-R1-Zero

Results

So, does it work? Here are the results of DeepSeek-R1-Zero. It made a lot of noise, because it was trained with pure RL. Directly from the base model, without any supervised fine-tuning. And yet, it reached a performance comparable to OpenAI's o1 reasoning model. Look at the plot: the accuracy on AIME 2024, a hard math competition, during training. The blue curve is pass at one: it goes from about 16 percent to 71 percent. The red curve uses majority voting over several samples: it reaches about 87 percent. The dashed lines are o1-0912: 74 percent, and 83 percent with majority voting. In the table, R1-Zero is better on some benchmarks, like MATH-500, and worse on others, like coding. And a fascinating detail: during training, its answers got longer and longer. The model learned by itself to think longer, and to re-check its work.
