Categorical DQN (C51)

Introduction & Motivation

Classic DQN:

  • Learns only the expectation $Q(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t \mid s,a\right]$
  • Bellman: \(Q(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\left[R(s,a) + \gamma Q(S', A')\right]\).
  • Lost information: variance, skewness, tail (risk) structure.

Distributional RL:

  • Model full return random variable \(Z(s,a)\) (not just its mean).
  • Distributional Bellman: \(\mathcal{T}^\pi Z(s,a) \stackrel{D}{=} R(s,a) + \gamma Z(S', A')\).
  • Benefits: richer training signal, more stable gradients, enables risk-sensitive objectives.

Key idea: preserve probabilistic structure throughout the backup,
then take \(\mathbb{E}[Z]\) only for action selection.

In this part, we focus on one algorithm: C51, also called Categorical DQN. Let's start from DQN. DQN learns Q of s, a: the expected sum of discounted rewards. And the Bellman equation links this expectation to the expectation at the next step. But the return is a random variable. The same state and action can lead to very different outcomes. When we keep only the mean, we lose a lot: the variance, the skewness, the tails... in other words, the risk. Distributional RL keeps all of it. We model the full random return: Z of s, a. And the Bellman equation still holds, but now in distribution: that's the D above the equal sign. The return here has the same distribution as the reward, plus gamma times the return at the next step. Why bother? A richer training signal, more stable learning, and risk-sensitive objectives become possible. And the key idea, at the bottom: we keep the whole distribution during learning. And we take its mean only at the very end, to choose the action.

DQN Recap

  • Goal: approximate \( Q^*(s,a) = \max_\pi \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\sum_{t} \gamma^t r_t \mid s,a\right] \)
  • Network: input is state, output is vector \( Q(s,a) \) for all actions
  • Bellman backup (target): \( y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') \)
  • Loss: mean squared error \( (y - Q(s,a))^2 \)
  • Limitation: collapses the whole distribution of returns into a single scalar value (its mean)
A quick recap of DQN, since C51 is built on top of it. The goal: approximate the optimal Q-function, Q star. The network takes the state as input: here, the Atari screen. A few convolutional layers, then fully connected layers. And the output is a vector: one Q-value per action, one for each joystick move. To train it, we build a target with the Bellman equation. The reward, plus gamma times the best Q-value at the next state. And we minimize the squared error between this target and our prediction. So it's basically a regression on a single number. And that's precisely the limitation. Whatever happens after this action, all the possible futures are squeezed into one number: their mean.

Distributional RL

  • Random return: \( Z(s,a) \) is a random variable over \( \mathbb{R} \)
  • Distributional Bellman: \( \mathcal{T}^\pi Z(s,a) \stackrel{D}{=} R(s,a) + \gamma Z(S', A') \), i.e., the law is transformed
  • Goal: approximate the law of \( Z(s,a) \) (not just \( \mathbb{E}[Z] \))
  • Expectation recoverable: \( Q(s,a) = \mathbb{E}[Z(s,a)] \)
  • C51: A Distributional Perspective on Reinforcement Learning, Bellemare et al., 2017.
Here is the picture to keep in mind. On the left, DQN: for each action, the output is a single point, the expected return. On the right, C51: for each action, the output is a whole histogram over the possible returns. Same feature extractor: only the output layer changes. So now, Z of s, a is a random variable, and we want to learn its law. And we don't lose anything: the Q-value is simply the mean of Z. On the theory side, Bellemare and colleagues proved a nice result. For a fixed policy, the distributional Bellman operator is a contraction, in the Wasserstein metric. So, like in classic RL, repeated updates converge to the true return distribution. But be careful: that's only for policy evaluation. For control, with the max, it's not a contraction anymore. Only the mean is guaranteed to converge. And C51 doesn't even minimize the Wasserstein distance: it uses a cross-entropy loss, as we'll see. So the theory doesn't fully cover C51... but it works very well in practice.

Categorical Approximation (C51)

  • Fixed discrete support: \( \{z_i\}_{i=0}^{N-1} \), with \( N = 51 \)
  • Bounded interval: \( [V_{\min}, V_{\max}] \)
  • \( \Delta z = \frac{V_{\max} - V_{\min}}{N-1} \)
  • Network predicts logits \( \to \) softmax \( \to \) \( p_i(s,a) \)
  • Approximate distribution: \( \mathbb{P}(Z(s,a) = z_i) = p_i \)
Choix fixe du support simplifie projection mais introduit rigidité si queue hors bornes.
Now, how do we represent a distribution with a neural network? C51 uses the simplest possible option: a histogram. We fix a grid of possible return values, between V min and V max. These values are called atoms: z i. There are 51 of them, evenly spaced, so the gap between two atoms is delta z. That's where the name comes from: C51, categorical with 51 atoms. On Atari, the paper uses V min equals minus 10, and V max equals plus 10. The atoms are fixed: the network doesn't move them. What it learns is the probability of each atom. For each action, it outputs 51 numbers, and a softmax turns them into probabilities that sum to one. The nice thing with a fixed grid: the loss becomes a simple cross-entropy, like in classification. The price: the bounds are a hyperparameter, and any return outside them is just clipped.

C51 Architecture

  • Backbone identical to DQN: convolutional layers followed by fully connected layers.
  • Output: $(\#\text{actions} \times N)$ logits, reshaped to $(\#\text{actions}, N)$.
  • Softmax over atoms for each action: $p_i(s,a)$.
  • Action selection: $a^* = \arg\max_a \sum_i p_i(s,a) z_i$.
  • Target: uses frozen (target) network and projection onto fixed support.
Now, the architecture. Here is DQN, in more detail. On the left, the network: a stack of the last 4 frames, 3 convolutional layers, and 2 fully connected layers. The output has N A values: one per action. On the right, the two networks of DQN. The online network, trained on samples from the replay buffer. And the target network, a copy that is updated only from time to time. [click] And here is C51. As you can see, it's exactly the same network, except for the last layer. Instead of N A outputs, we have N A times m: m numbers per action, with m equal to 51. We reshape them into a matrix, with one row per action. And we apply a softmax on each row, to get one distribution per action. By the way, QR-DQN, that we'll see later, uses the same layout. To act, we compute the mean of each distribution: the sum of z i times p i. And we take the action with the highest mean. As in DQN, the targets are computed with a frozen target network.

But wait...

Why model the full value distribution if action selection is based only on the expected value?

But wait... something is strange here. In the end, C51 chooses its actions with the mean, exactly like DQN. So why bother learning the whole distribution? Let's go back to Space Invaders. For a laser action, there are two possible outcomes. Either we lose, with a return around 0. Or we survive, with a return around 6 to 9. DQN only learns the mean: something like 5. But a return of 5 never happens: it's an "unrealizable" value. The usual intuition: one sample pulls the estimate toward 0, the next one toward 8. So the targets are blurry, and the gradients contradict each other. C51 keeps the two modes separated: no averaging, no conflict. So the usual answer is: a richer learning signal, since each update touches all the atoms. And more stable learning. But to be honest, the exact reason is still debated. Lyle and colleagues showed in 2019 that, in the tabular and linear cases, distributional RL behaves exactly like classic RL. So the gain seems to come from the interaction with deep networks.

DQN Algorithm

Input:
$\quad\quad$ none
Algorithm parameter:
$\quad\quad$ discount factor $\gamma$
$\quad\quad$ step size $\alpha \in (0,1]$
$\quad\quad$ small $\epsilon > 0$
$\quad\quad$ capacity of the experience replay memory $M$
$\quad\quad$ batch size $m$
$\quad\quad$ target network update frequency $\tau$

Initialize replay memory $\mathcal{D}$ to capacity $M$
Initialize action-value function $\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_1}}$ with random weights $\mathbf{\omega_1}$
Initialize target action-value function $\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_2}}$ with weights $\mathbf{\omega_2} = \mathbf{\omega_1}$

FOR EACH episode
$\quad$ $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \text{env.reset}()$
$\quad$ DO
$\quad\quad$ $\mathbf{a} \leftarrow$ $\epsilon\text{-greedy}(\mathbf{s}, \hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_1}})$
$\quad\quad$ $r, \mathbf{s'} \leftarrow \text{env.step}(\mathbf{a})$
$\quad\quad$ Store transition $(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}, r, \mathbf{s'})$ in $\mathcal{D}$
$\quad\quad$ If $\mathcal{D}$ contains "enough" transitions
$\quad\quad\quad$ Sample random batch of transitions $(\mathbf{s}_j, \mathbf{a}_j, r_j, \mathbf{s'}_j)$ from $\mathcal{D}$ with $j=1$ to $m$
$\quad\quad\quad$ For each $j$, set $y_j = \begin{cases} r_j & \text{for terminal } \mathbf{s'}_j\\ r_j + \gamma \max_{\mathbf{a}^\star} \hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_{2}}} (\mathbf{s'}_j)_{\mathbf{a}^\star} & \text{for non-terminal } \mathbf{s'}_j \end{cases}$
$\quad\quad\quad$ Perform a gradient descent step on $\left( y_j - \hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_1}}(\mathbf{s}_j)_{\mathbf{a}_j} \right)^2$ with respect to the weights $\mathbf{\omega_1}$
$\quad\quad\quad$ Every $\tau$ steps reset $\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_2}}$ to $\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_1}}$, i.e., set $\mathbf{\omega_2} \leftarrow \mathbf{\omega_1}$
$\quad\quad$ $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \mathbf{s'}$
$\quad$ UNTIL $\mathbf{s}$ is final

RETURN $\mathbf{\omega_1}$
Before we look at the C51 algorithm, let's recall the DQN algorithm, that you already know. I just want to point out the three places that C51 will change. [click] First, the action selection: ε-greedy, based on the Q-values. Second, the target, y j: the reward, plus gamma times the max Q-value at the next state, given by the target network. Third, the loss: a squared error between this target and the prediction. Everything else stays the same in C51. The replay memory, the mini-batches, and the target network, updated every tau steps.

C51 Algorithm

FOR EACH episode
$\quad$ $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \text{env.reset}()$
$\quad$ DO
$\quad\quad$ $\mathbf{a} \leftarrow$ $\epsilon\text{-greedy}(\mathbf{s}, \hat{Z}_{\mathbf{\omega_1}})$
$\quad\quad$ $r, \mathbf{s'} \leftarrow \text{env.step}(\mathbf{a})$
$\quad\quad$ Store transition $(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}, r, \mathbf{s'})$ in $\mathcal{D}$
$\quad\quad$ IF $\mathcal{D}$ contains "enough" transitions
$\quad\quad\quad$ Sample random batch of transitions $(\mathbf{s}_j, \mathbf{a}_j, r_j, \mathbf{s'}_j)$ from $\mathcal{D}$ with $j=1$ to $m$
$\quad\quad\quad$ FOR EACH transition $(\mathbf{s}_j, \mathbf{a}_j, r_j, \mathbf{s'}_j)$ of this batch
$\quad\quad\quad\quad$ Apply the Bellman operator
$\quad\quad\quad\quad$ Compute the projection $\Phi$ of $\mathcal{T}^{\pi}Z$ onto the support $\{z_i\}$
$\quad\quad\quad\quad$ Perform a gradient descent step to minimize the cross-entropy
$\quad\quad\quad$ Every $\tau$ steps reset $\hat{Z}_{\mathbf{\omega_2}}$ to $\hat{Z}_{\mathbf{\omega_1}}$, i.e., set $\mathbf{\omega_2} \leftarrow \mathbf{\omega_1}$
$\quad\quad$ $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \mathbf{s'}$
$\quad$ UNTIL $\mathbf{s}$ is final

RETURN $\mathbf{\omega_1}$

$$Q(s,a) = \mathbb{E}[Z(s,a)] = \sum_i z_i \, p_i(s,a).$$ The value $Q$ can be obtained as the expectation of $Z$.

Behavior policy: with probability $1-\epsilon$, take the action that maximizes the expected value
according to the current distributional estimate.

Since the support of the target distribution does not necessarily fall exactly on the fixed support of the predicted distribution,
we project it using the operator $(\Phi)$.

Perform a gradient descent step to minimize the cross-entropy between
the predicted distribution and the target distribution. $$L(\omega) = - \sum_i p_i^{\text{target}} \log p_i^{\text{pred}}$$

Now, the C51 algorithm: the overall structure is exactly the same as DQN. [click] First change: the networks now output distributions, Z hat, instead of Q-values. But we can always get a Q-value back: it's just the mean, the sum of z i times p i. [click] So the behavior policy doesn't change: it's still ε-greedy, based on this mean. [click] Then, we build the target with the distributional Bellman operator, using the target network. We take the distribution of the next state, and we multiply it by gamma: it shrinks toward zero. And we add the reward: the distribution is shifted. [click] Now, the bars don't fall on our 51 atoms anymore. So we project them back onto the fixed grid: each bar is split between its two nearest atoms. [click] Finally, we minimize the cross-entropy between this target and our prediction, instead of the squared error of DQN.

Atari Results (Summary)

  • Improved median human-normalized score: 178% vs 79% for DQN
  • Faster learning: within 50M frames, outperforms a fully trained DQN on 45 of 57 games
  • More stable learning (according to the authors)
  • Foundation for Rainbow (integration of further improvements)
So, does it work? Yes, and quite impressively. This chart shows, for each Atari game, the improvement of C51 over Double DQN. Note that it's a log scale. On most games, the bar is positive, and on some games the gain is huge: thousands of percent. On a few games, C51 is worse, down to about minus 40 percent. Overall, the median human-normalized score goes from 79% for DQN, to 178% for C51. It also learns faster. After only 50 million frames, it beats a fully trained DQN on 45 of the 57 games. And the authors also report more stable learning. Finally, C51 became one of the building blocks of Rainbow. Rainbow combines several improvements of DQN in a single agent, including the Noisy Nets we saw earlier.

C51

Limitations & Extensions

  • Fixed support: risk of saturation if returns fall outside $[V_{\min}, V_{\max}]$
  • Uniform resolution: inefficient for modeling sharp tails or skewed distributions
  • Extensions:
    • QR-DQN: learns the locations of quantiles (adaptive support: no $[V_{\min}, V_{\max}]$ to choose, no heuristic projection)
    • IQN: continuous implicit quantile function for flexible modeling
  • Paradigm: working with distributions enables risk-sensitive and utility-based RL
Of course, C51 has some limitations. First, the support is fixed. You must choose V min and V max in advance. If the true returns fall outside this range, the distribution just saturates at the bounds. Second, the resolution is uniform: the 51 atoms are evenly spaced. So we waste atoms where there is no mass. And we lack precision where the distribution is sharp, or in the tails. Plus, the projection step is a bit of a heuristic. This motivated several extensions. QR-DQN flips the idea. Instead of learning probabilities on fixed locations, it learns the locations of quantiles. So the support adapts by itself: no V min, no V max, no projection. IQN goes one step further: it learns the whole quantile function, as a continuous function. And more generally, once we have the distribution, we can optimize other things than the mean. That's risk-sensitive RL.

Conclusion & Discussion

  • Moving from expectation to distribution provides a richer learning signal.
  • C51 is simple, effective, and forms the basis for many improvements.
  • Actions are selected via the expectation: the full distribution is valuable for training and for future risk-sensitive objectives.
  • Enables risk-sensitive RL, robustness, and informed exploration.
  • Open questions: how to choose the support? How to combine with epistemic uncertainty?
So, to sum up C51. The core idea: move from the expectation to the full distribution of returns. It's a simple change: just the output layer and the loss. And it gives a richer learning signal. C51 is simple, it works well, and many later methods build on it. Remember the paradox: we still act on the mean. The distribution helps during training, and it's ready for risk-sensitive objectives. Some open questions. How do we choose the support? Well, that's partly what quantile methods answer. And how do we combine it with epistemic uncertainty? Remember the noisy TV. The spread of Z measures the randomness of the environment, not what the agent doesn't know yet. These are two different kinds of uncertainty, and good exploration needs the second one.

Quantile Regression Deep Q-Network

  • Represents the value distribution by estimating the values of $N$ quantiles at fixed probability levels (the reverse of C51: fixed probabilities, learned locations).
  • Uses quantile regression loss to train the network.
  • Improves stability and expressiveness over categorical methods.

Distributional Reinforcement Learning with Quantile Regression, Dabney et al., 2017.

Let's look at QR-DQN a bit more closely. This figure compares the three outputs. DQN: one point per action. C51: fixed atoms on the return axis, and the network learns their heights, the probabilities. QR-DQN: it's the reverse. The probabilities are fixed: each output carries the same mass, one over N. And the network learns where to put them on the return axis. So each output is a quantile of the return distribution. For example, with N equal to 4, the outputs are the 12.5, 37.5, 62.5 and 87.5 percent quantiles. To learn quantiles, we use the quantile regression loss: an asymmetric absolute error. In practice, a smoothed version: the quantile Huber loss. Result: no bounds to choose, no projection, and better scores than C51 on Atari. And it brings theory and practice closer. Quantile regression is linked to the Wasserstein distance: the one where the Bellman operator is a contraction.

Extensions

QR with Utility / Risk-sensitive RL

  • Integrate utility functions into quantile-based RL
    for risk-sensitive objectives.
  • Customize agent behavior: risk-averse, risk-seeking,
    or robust policies.
  • Applications in finance, robotics, and safety-critical domains.
And this opens a whole new world: risk-sensitive RL. Once we have the quantiles, we're not forced to maximize the mean anymore. We can plug in a utility function, or a risk measure. For instance, a risk-averse agent can focus on the worst cases. Say, the average of the 10% worst returns. That's called the CVaR: the conditional value at risk. A risk-seeking agent does the opposite: it looks at the best cases. IQN makes this especially easy: it can output any quantile, so we just choose which ones to look at when we act. Where is it useful? Everywhere a bad outcome is very costly. In finance: we care about the risk of a big loss, not only about the average gain. In robotics and safety-critical systems. A robot that's great on average, but crashes once in a while... is not a great robot.
