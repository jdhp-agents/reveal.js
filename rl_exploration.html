Exploration-Exploitation Dilemma

  • Agent must explore to discover rewarding actions and states.
  • Agent must exploit known information to maximize reward.
  • Balancing exploration and exploitation is critical for efficient learning.
  • Too much exploration: slow progress; too much exploitation: suboptimal policies.
Let's start with a picture. Here is our robot, and it's hungry. On the left, "The Usual Place": a restaurant it knows well, and it's pretty happy with it. On the right, a new restaurant just opened. So, what should it do? If it goes to the usual place, it gets a good meal for sure. That's exploitation: use what you already know to get the best reward. If it tries the new place, maybe it's disappointing... or maybe it's a new favorite. That's exploration: take some risk to gather information. Well, an RL agent has exactly the same problem. It must explore to discover rewarding actions and states. And it must exploit what it knows to maximize its reward. The hard part is to find the right balance. Too much exploration, and learning is slow: we keep trying bad options. Too much exploitation, and we get stuck with a suboptimal policy: we never find the better restaurant. The best long-term strategy often means accepting some short-term sacrifices.

Unstructured Exploration

$\varepsilon$-greedy

Deep Q-Learning Algorithm
FOR EACH episode
$\quad$ $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \text{env.reset}()$
$\quad$ DO
$\quad\quad$ $\mathbf{a} \leftarrow \color{red}{\epsilon\text{-greedy}(\mathbf{s}, \hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_1}})}$
$\quad\quad$ $r, \mathbf{s'} \leftarrow \text{env.step}(\mathbf{a})$
$\quad\quad$ Store transition $(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}, r, \mathbf{s'})$ in $\mathcal{D}$
$\quad\quad$ If $\mathcal{D}$ contains "enough" transitions
$\quad\quad\quad$ Sample random batch of transitions $(\mathbf{s}_j, \mathbf{a}_j, r_j, \mathbf{s'}_j)$ from $\mathcal{D}$ with $j=1$ to $m$
$\quad\quad\quad$ For each $j$, set $y_j = \begin{cases} r_j & \text{for terminal } \mathbf{s'}_j\\ r_j + \gamma \max_{\mathbf{a}^\star} \hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_{2}}} (\mathbf{s'}_j)_{\mathbf{a}^\star} & \text{for non-terminal } \mathbf{s'}_j \end{cases}$
$\quad\quad\quad$ Perform a gradient descent step on $\left( y_j - \hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_1}}(\mathbf{s}_j)_{\mathbf{a}_j} \right)^2$ with respect to the weights $\mathbf{\omega_1}$
$\quad\quad\quad$ Every $\tau$ steps reset $\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_2}}$ to $\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{\omega_1}}$, i.e., set $\mathbf{\omega_2} \leftarrow \mathbf{\omega_1}$
$\quad\quad$ $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \mathbf{s'}$
$\quad$ UNTIL $\mathbf{s}$ is final
RETURN $\mathbf{\omega_1}$

At each step:

  • with probability ε, select a random action;
  • with probability 1-ε, select the action with the highest estimated value.

👉 Simple to implement, widely used in discrete action spaces.

Typically, ε is annealed (decreased) over time to shift from exploration to exploitation.
The decay schedule is a hyperparameter and often determined empirically.

So, how do we explore in practice? The simplest family of methods is called unstructured exploration. "Unstructured" means we just inject some randomness into the agent's choices. The best-known example is ε-greedy. You already know it: it's the line in red, in the DQN algorithm. At each step, with probability ε, we pick a random action. Otherwise, we pick the greedy action: the one with the highest estimated Q-value. It's very simple, and it's the default choice in discrete action spaces. [click] In practice, ε is not constant. We start with a large ε, so the agent explores a lot. Then we decrease ε over time, down to a small minimum value. So the agent shifts smoothly from exploration to exploitation. How fast should ε decrease? Well, that's a hyperparameter, and it's usually tuned by trial and error. The weakness of ε-greedy: this randomness is blind. The agent doesn't try to go where it knows the least.

Unstructured Exploration

Gaussian Noise

  • Add zero-mean Gaussian noise to the action output by the policy.
  • Common in algorithms for continuous control (e.g., DDPG, TD3).
  • Noise variance can be annealed to reduce exploration over time.
  • Encourages local exploration around the current policy's actions.

$$\mu'(s_t) = \mu(s_t | \theta^{\mu}_t) + \mathcal{N}$$ where $\mu(s_t | \theta^{\mu}_t)$ is the actor network with weights $\theta^{\mu}_t$
and $\mathcal{N}$ is a noise process (e.g. Gaussian or Ornstein-Uhlenbeck).

Now, what about continuous actions? Here, ε-greedy is not the natural choice. Instead, we take the action of the policy, and we add some noise to it. Look at the formula. μ is the actor network: it outputs a deterministic action. And we add a noise term, N, on top of it. Typically, a zero-mean Gaussian noise. That's what DDPG and TD3 do. And like with ε, we can reduce the noise over time. A small detail. Independent Gaussian noise makes the actions jitter from one step to the next. So the original DDPG paper used an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. It's a noise that is correlated in time, better for physical systems with inertia. Later, TD3 showed that simple Gaussian noise works just as well in practice. The limit? This exploration is local: the agent only tries actions close to what it already does. So it won't discover far-away or rare states.

Limits of Unstructured Exploration

Case Study: Montezuma's Revenge (Atari)

Unstructured methods (ε-greedy, Gaussian noise) struggle in environments with sparse rewards and complex exploration requirements.

Montezuma's Revenge (Atari): classic benchmark for hard exploration.

  • Random actions rarely reach rewarding states; agent fails to learn meaningful behavior.
  • Motivates the need for intrinsic motivation and structured exploration techniques.
OK, so do these simple methods work? Well, on easy problems, yes. But here is a famous counter-example: Montezuma's Revenge, an Atari game. [play the video] To get the first reward, the player must grab the key. So: go down the ladders, jump over the rolling skull, and climb up to the key. And to get the next reward, go all the way back and open a door. That's a long and very precise sequence of actions. One bad jump, and you lose a life. Now imagine an agent that just presses random buttons. It will almost never reach the key. So it almost never sees a reward: the rewards are sparse. And with no reward, there is nothing to learn. In fact, the original DQN scored zero points on this game. So we need smarter ways to explore. That's the motivation for the rest of this part: intrinsic motivation, and structured exploration.

Maximum Entropy RL

Exploration and Robustness via Entropy

Augments the reward with an entropy term to encourage diverse actions: $$ J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi} \left[ \sum_t \Big( r_t + \alpha \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|s_t)) \Big) \right] $$ where $\mathcal{H}$ is the entropy and $\alpha$ controls the exploration-exploitation tradeoff.

  • Promotes stochastic policies, improving robustness and exploration.
  • Widely used in robotics and continuous action domains.
Another family of methods: maximum entropy RL. Jesse already presented it in the first part of the course, so I'll be quick. Here, the idea is to change the objective itself. Look at the formula. At each step, the agent gets the usual reward, r. Plus a bonus: the entropy of its policy in the current state, weighted by α. What does it mean? Entropy measures how random the policy is. So the agent is rewarded for keeping its options open. For not committing too early to a single action. And α sets the balance: a large α means more exploration, and with α equal to zero, we're back to standard RL. Two benefits. First, better exploration, since the policy stays stochastic. Second, more robust policies: the agent learns several ways to solve the task, not just one. That's why it's widely used in robotics and continuous control.

Maximum Entropy RL

Exploration and Robustness via Entropy

Key algorithms:

  • Soft Q-Learning (SQL): incorporates entropy into Q-learning updates.
    Reinforcement Learning with Deep Energy-Based Policies, Haarnoja et al., 2017.
  • Soft Actor-Critic (SAC): off-policy actor-critic with entropy maximization,
    a standard baseline for continuous control.
    Soft Actor-Critic: Off-Policy Maximum Entropy Deep Reinforcement Learning with a Stochastic Actor, Haarnoja et al., 2018.
Two key algorithms here, both by Haarnoja and colleagues. First, Soft Q-Learning, in 2017. It puts the entropy term inside the Q-learning update. That's why it's called "soft". The hard max over actions of Q-learning becomes a soft maximum: a log-sum-exp. Then, Soft Actor-Critic, or SAC, in 2018. It's an off-policy actor-critic, with this entropy bonus. Off-policy means it can reuse past experience from a replay buffer. So it's quite sample-efficient. SAC quickly became a standard baseline for continuous control. If you need a solid algorithm for a robot arm, SAC is often the first thing to try.

Parameter Noise-based Exploration

Noisy Nets, Parameter Space Noise

  • Fortunato et al. (2018). Noisy Networks for Exploration
  • Plappert et al. (2018). Parameter space noise for exploration
Next idea: instead of adding noise to the actions, we add noise to the network's weights. Two papers proposed this, at the same time. Why is it different? With action noise, every step is perturbed independently. So the agent keeps changing its mind, at every single step. With parameter noise, we perturb the policy itself. As long as the perturbation is fixed, the behavior is consistent: same state, same action. It's like trying a slightly different strategy, rather than pressing random buttons. In Noisy Nets, the linear layers of the network get noisy weights. And the network learns the amount of noise, by gradient descent. So there is no ε to tune anymore: the agent decides by itself how much to explore. By the way, Noisy Nets is one of the components of Rainbow. Parameter space noise, by Plappert and colleagues, applies the same idea to DQN, DDPG and TRPO. Here, the noise is sampled at the start of an episode, and kept fixed during the episode. And its scale is adapted automatically, to keep a target level of change in the actions.

Intrinsic Reward-based Exploration

Augment environment reward $r_t$ with an intrinsic reward $r^i_t$: $$r_t \leftarrow r_t + \beta \cdot r^i_t$$ where $\beta$ balances exploitation vs exploration.

👉 Encourages agent to explore novel or uncertain states.

Two main approaches:

  • Count-based exploration: bonus for visiting rarely seen states.
  • Prediction-based exploration: bonus for high prediction error (surprise).
Let's come back to our Montezuma problem: sparse rewards. If the environment almost never gives a reward... well, let's create our own! The idea: we add an intrinsic reward to the reward of the environment. The environment reward is called extrinsic: it defines the task. The intrinsic reward is an exploration bonus: it's high when the agent sees something new. And β is a weight, to balance exploitation and exploration. This comes from psychology: intrinsic motivation, or curiosity. Think of a child: nobody pays them to explore, exploring is rewarding in itself. So now, the question is: how do we measure "new"? There are two main approaches. Count-based exploration: a bonus for states we have rarely visited. Prediction-based exploration: a bonus when the agent is surprised, when it fails to predict what happens. Let's see both.

Intrinsic Reward-based Exploration

Count-based Exploration

Assigns an exploration bonus that decreases with the state visit count $N(s_t)$: $$r^i_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N(s_t)}}$$

Advantage: Effective in small, discrete state spaces.

Limitation: Infeasible in high-dimensional or continuous spaces
(counts become sparse or meaningless).

Alternatives: approximate state visitation counts:

  • Bellemare et al., 2016 – CTS-based pseudocounts
  • Ostrovski et al., 2017 – PixelCNN-based pseudocounts
  • Tang et al., 2017 – Hash-based counts
The first approach is the most intuitive one: we count. N of s is the number of times we have visited state s. And the bonus is one over the square root of this count. So a new state gives a big bonus, and the more we visit it, the smaller the bonus. This pushes the agent toward rarely visited states. In a small, discrete world, like a grid world, this works very well. But what about Atari, where the state is an image? Well, you almost never see the exact same image twice. So almost all the counts are zero, or one. And the bonus doesn't tell us anything useful. Same problem with continuous states. So the solution is to approximate the counts: we call them pseudo-counts. Bellemare and colleagues use a density model of the images, to estimate how familiar a state is. Ostrovski and colleagues improve it with a neural density model, PixelCNN. And Tang and colleagues use a simpler trick: they hash each state into a short code, and count the codes. So similar states share the same count.

Intrinsic Reward-based Exploration

Prediction-based Exploration

Uses prediction error as intrinsic reward: $$r^i_t = \| f(s_t, a_t) - s_{t+1} \|$$ where $f$ is a learned forward dynamics model.

Properties:

  • Agent is rewarded for visiting states where its model is uncertain or inaccurate (novelty/surprise).
  • Common in high-dimensional or continuous environments.

Limitations:

  • Model can be "fooled" by stochasticity or noise.
  • May focus on unpredictable but irrelevant states.
  • Motivates use of random networks (e.g., RND) for more robust novelty estimation.
The second approach: we reward the agent when it is surprised. For that, the agent learns a forward model, f. It takes the current state and the action, and it tries to predict the next state. The intrinsic reward is the prediction error: the gap between the prediction and what really happened. Why does it work? If the model predicts well, the agent already knows this part of the world: no bonus. If the error is large, it's an unknown region: big bonus, so let's go there! And as the model learns, the bonus decreases: same effect as with counts, but without counting. That's why it scales to images and continuous states. In practice, like in ICM, we usually predict features of the next state, rather than raw pixels. But there is a catch. The model can be fooled by randomness. If something in the environment is just unpredictable, the error stays high forever. So the agent may focus on things that are unpredictable, but completely irrelevant. Intrinsic Reward-based Exploration

Limits of Prediction-based Intrinsic Motivation

  • In stochastic environments, dynamics are unpredictable (e.g., random noise, a "noisy TV" showing random images).
  • Prediction error remains high even for familiar states.
  • Agent is attracted to noisy or unpredictable regions, leading to biased and inefficient exploration.
This is called the noisy TV problem. Imagine there is a TV in the maze, and it shows random images. The agent tries to predict the next image on the screen. But it's pure noise: it can never predict it. Even after watching it for hours. So the prediction error stays high, and the bonus stays high. Result: the agent is hooked. It stays in front of the TV forever, like a couch potato. And it stops exploring the rest of the world. So, the problem is this. The prediction error mixes up two very different things. Something new, that we can learn about. And something just random, that we will never be able to predict. In technical terms, the first one is epistemic uncertainty: what we don't know yet. The second one is aleatoric uncertainty: the pure randomness of the environment. For exploration, we want a bonus for the first one only.

Intrinsic Reward-based Exploration

Motivation for RND

A measure of novelty that is more stable and less sensitive to randomness.

  • Target network = a fixed random function \( f_{\text{target}}(s) \).
    • Since it is frozen, it defines a stable representation of states.
    • Each state has a deterministic, time-invariant signature.
  • Predictor network = a network trained to approximate \( f_{\text{target}}(s) \).
    • The prediction error is high at first for all states.
    • As some states are revisited, the predictor learns to approximate them → error decreases.
    • Truly novel states remain hard → high error → strong intrinsic reward.

👉 Unlike dynamics prediction, this error does not depend on the environment’s stochasticity, but only on whether the state has already been “learned” by the predictor.

So here comes Random Network Distillation, or RND. It keeps the idea of a prediction error. But it changes what we predict. We take a first network, initialized at random, and we freeze it. That's the target network. It never learns anything: it's just a fixed, random function of the state. So each state gets a kind of random signature, and this signature never changes. Then, a second network, the predictor, is trained to reproduce this signature. At the beginning, the predictor is bad everywhere: the error is high for all states. On the states we visit often, it gets good: the error goes down. But on new states, it has never been trained: the error stays high. So this error is a measure of novelty. And here is the key point. The target only depends on the current state, not on what happens next. It's a deterministic function of the state. So the randomness of the transitions can't fool it anymore.

Intrinsic Reward-based Exploration

Focus on RND

  • RND uses two neural networks:
    • Target network: randomly initialized, fixed parameters.
    • Predictor network: trained to predict the target network's output for each state.
  • Intrinsic reward: prediction error (difference between predictor and target outputs).
    • States that are novel (rarely visited) yield high prediction error and thus high intrinsic reward.
    • As the agent visits a state more often, the predictor learns to match the target, reducing the intrinsic reward.
    • Encourages exploration of unfamiliar states.

Burda et al. (2018). Exploration by random network distillation.

So, let me summarize how RND works. Two networks, and both take the state as input. The target: random weights, frozen. The predictor: trained to match the target's output, on the states we visit. The intrinsic reward is the gap between the two outputs. New state: big gap, big reward. Familiar state: small gap, small reward. Why is it nice? First, it's simple: two standard networks, and no model of the environment's dynamics. Second, it's stable: the target never moves, so the definition of novelty doesn't drift. Third, it's much less fooled by randomness, as we just saw. And it works! RND was the first method to beat the average human score on Montezuma's Revenge. Without any human demonstration.
