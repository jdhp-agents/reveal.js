Hello everyone. Today, we are going to study Proximal Policy Optimization, or PPO. It is probably the most widely used policy gradient algorithm today. It trains robots, it plays video games, and it was used to fine-tune ChatGPT. You already know TD actor-critic. So we will start from there, and add one ingredient at a time, until we get PPO.

Here is the plan, and the road we will follow. We start from TD actor-critic. We will see that it has four weaknesses. Each weakness calls for one new ingredient, with its own color. In green, rollouts and GAE, to get better advantage estimates. In orange, data reuse, with an importance ratio. In purple, clipping, to keep each policy step small. Starting point: one-step TD Actor-Critic

Input: policy $\pi(a|s,\bth)$, critic $\vhat(s,\bw)$
Initialize $\bth$ and $\bw$ (e.g. to $0$)
Loop for each episode:
$S \leftarrow$ first state of episode ; $I \leftarrow 1$
Loop while $S$ is not terminal:
$A \sim \pi(\cdot|S,\bth)$
Take action $A$, observe $S', R$
$\delta \leftarrow R + \gamma \vhat(S',\bw) - \vhat(S,\bw)$
$\bw \leftarrow \bw + \alpha^{\bw} \, \delta \, \nabla \vhat(S,\bw)$
$\bth \leftarrow \bth + \alpha^{\bth} I \, \delta \, \nabla \ln \pi(A|S,\bth)$
$I \leftarrow \gamma I$ ; $S \leftarrow S'$

Sutton & Barto (2018), §13.5. Here: tabular, $\vhat(s,\bw) = w_s$, $\pi(\cdot|s,\bth) = \operatorname{softmax}(\theta_{s,\cdot})$, $\alpha^{\bth} = 1$, $\alpha^{\bw} = 0.5$, $\gamma = 0.9$.

Rappel de TD actor-critic (Sutton & Barto) sur un couloir : petite récompense proche à gauche (+0.5), grande récompense loin à droite (+1). Premier pas : δ = 0, rien n'est appris. Deuxième pas : la récompense arrive, δ = 0.5. Épisode 2 : l'information ne remonte que d'une case par visite. Après 1000 pas, l'agent est piégé à gauche.
Let's start with what you know: one-step TD actor-critic, as in Sutton and Barto. Our toy problem is a corridor. On the left, a small reward, plus 0.5, close to the start. On the right, a big reward, plus 1, but further away. The arrows show the policy, and the numbers below show the critic. [click × 6: first transition] We go left, from 2 to 1, with no reward. The critic knows nothing yet, so delta is zero: nothing is learned. [click × 6: second transition] Now we reach the left goal: delta is 0.5. The critic of state 1 goes up, and the policy in state 1 prefers left. [click × 2: next episode] In the next episode, delta is positive in state 2: the information moved back one state only. [click] After a thousand steps, the agent is trapped by the small reward on the left.

$\delta_t$ is an estimate of the advantage

Advantage of action $a$ in state $s$: how much better $a$ is than what $\pi$ does on average $$\Adv_\pi(s,a) \doteq q_\pi(s,a) - v_\pi(s)$$

If the critic is exact ($\vhat = v_\pi$), the TD error is an unbiased estimate of it: $$\E_\pi\big[\delta_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a\big] = \underbrace{\E_\pi\big[R_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid s, a\big]}_{q_\pi(s,a)} - \, v_\pi(s) = \Adv_\pi(s,a)$$

The actor update is a stochastic gradient step on $J(\bth)$, with the policy gradient theorem (with baseline): $$\nabla J(\bth) \propto \E_\pi\big[\Adv_\pi(S_t,A_t) \, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t|S_t,\bth)\big] \qquad \text{TD AC: } \Adv_\pi(S_t,A_t) \approx \delta_t$$

Notation. $\hAdv_t$ = any estimate of $\Adv_\pi(S_t,A_t)$; $\delta_t$ is the simplest one. We write $\Adv$, not $A$: in Sutton & Barto, $A_t$ is the action. (The PPO paper writes $\hat{A}_t$ for $\hAdv_t$, and $r_t(\theta)$ for the ratio $\rho_t(\bth)$ we will meet soon.)
L'avantage Adv = q − v ; si le critique est exact, δ_t en est un estimateur sans biais ; l'acteur fait donc une montée de gradient stochastique avec le théorème du gradient de politique avec ligne de base. Notation : Adv et non A (A_t est l'action chez Sutton & Barto).
Before we improve this algorithm, let's look at delta from a different angle. The advantage of an action is how much better it is than what the policy does on average. It is q minus v. [click] If the critic were exact, the expected TD error would be exactly the advantage. So delta is a one-sample estimate of the advantage. [click] And the actor update is a stochastic gradient step, from the policy gradient theorem. The advantage tells us in which direction to push the probability of each action. [click] A word on notation. We write Adv hat for any advantage estimate, because capital A is already the action. The PPO paper uses A hat and r of theta: be careful when you read it.

Four weaknesses of TD Actor-Critic

WeaknessIngredient of PPO
$\delta_t$ looks one step ahead: biased when $\vhat \neq v_\pi$, and rewards propagate back one state per visit Rollouts and GAE: look further ahead, tune bias vs variance
Each transition is used for one gradient step, then thrown away Data reuse: several epochs on each batch, with an importance ratio
Nothing but $\alpha^{\bth}$ limits how much the policy changes in one update Clipping: keep the new policy close to the old one
The policy can become deterministic too early and stop exploring Entropy bonus: keep the policy stochastic

Each ingredient fixes one weakness, and each one is simple: PPO = TD Actor-Critic + 4 small changes.

Quatre faiblesses de TD actor-critic, chacune associée à un ingrédient de PPO (code couleur conservé dans tout le deck).
So what is wrong with TD actor-critic? I see four weaknesses, one per ingredient of PPO. [click] First, delta looks only one step ahead. It is biased when the critic is wrong, and rewards travel back very slowly: we just saw it. [click] Second, each transition is used for one gradient step, then thrown away. [click] Third, only the step size limits how much the policy changes. This will become a real problem when we reuse data. [click] Fourth, the policy can become deterministic too early, like our agent stuck on the left. [click] Each ingredient fixes one weakness, and each one is simple. PPO is TD actor-critic plus four small changes.

Part 1

Better advantages: rollouts and GAE

Partie 1 : de meilleurs estimateurs de l'avantage.
Part one: how to get better advantage estimates than delta.

Looking further ahead: $n$-step advantages

$\hAdv^{(n)}_t \doteq G_{t:t+n} - \vhat(S_t,\bw) = \sum_{l=0}^{n-1} \gamma^l \delta_{t+l}$   with $G_{t:t+n} \doteq R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \dots + \gamma^{n-1} R_{t+n} + \gamma^n \vhat(S_{t+n},\bw)$ (telescoping sum)

One trajectory in the corridor, $\pi(\rightarrow|s) = 0.7$; the critic lags behind: $\vhat$ = values of the uniform policy.

Estimateurs à n pas : somme des n premiers δ actualisés = retour à n pas moins v̂(S_t). n = 1 : TD ; n = ∞ : Monte Carlo. Sur cette trajectoire, δ_0 est négatif alors que l'action était bonne : l'estimateur à un pas se trompe de signe.
So how can we look further ahead? Here is one trajectory in the corridor: we start in 2, we go right, and after six steps we reach the big reward. Below each transition, the bar is its TD error, delta t. [click] With n equal to one, the advantage estimate is just delta zero. It is slightly negative: TD says this action was bad. [click × 2] With n equal to two, then three, we add the next deltas, discounted by gamma. This sum telescopes into the n-step return, minus the value of S t. [click] With n equal to infinity, we use the whole return: this is Monte Carlo. Now the estimate is clearly positive: going right was a good idea. The one-step estimate even got the sign wrong, because the critic is out of date.

From online updates to rollouts

To compute $\hAdv^{(n)}_t$ at time $t$, we need $R_{t+1}, \dots, R_{t+n}$ and $S_{t+n}$: rewards from the future.

  • It makes GAE possible: when we compute the advantages, all the future $\delta$'s of the batch are known.
  • It provides a batch, collected by one fixed policy $\pi(\cdot|\cdot,\thold)$, that we could reuse (Part 2).
Passer de l'online au batch (rollouts de T pas, comme A2C) : c'est ce qui rend GAE possible (il faut connaître les δ futurs), et c'est aussi ce qui fournit le lot à réutiliser (partie 2).
But there is a practical problem. To look n steps ahead, we need rewards from the future. In TD actor-critic, we update after every transition, so the future is not known yet. [click] The solution: rollouts. We run the current policy for T steps, and we store everything in a buffer. This is how A2C works, often with several copies of the environment in parallel. [click] Then we compute all the advantages backwards, from the end of the buffer. It makes GAE possible: when we compute the advantages, all the future deltas of the batch are known. [click] And there is a second benefit, the key to PPO. We now have a whole batch, collected by one fixed policy, theta old. Why use it only once? This is Part 2.

Generalized Advantage Estimation: GAE($\lambda$)

$\hAdv_t \doteq (1-\lambda) \sum_{n \ge 1} \lambda^{n-1} \hAdv^{(n)}_t = \sum_{l \ge 0} (\gamma\lambda)^l \, \delta_{t+l} = G^\lambda_t - \vhat(S_t,\bw)$   (S&B ch. 12)

In practice, one backward pass over the batch:   $\hAdv_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda \, \hAdv_{t+1}$   (reset at the end of an episode)

GAE(λ) : moyenne exponentielle des estimateurs à n pas = somme des δ pondérés par (γλ)^l = λ-retour moins v̂. Calcul : une passe arrière sur le lot. λ = 0 : TD ; λ = 1 : Monte Carlo ; PPO : λ = 0.95. Le curseur permet de faire varier λ.
Which n should we choose? GAE answers: don't choose, take an exponential average of all of them. It is exactly the lambda-return of Sutton and Barto, minus the value of the state. And the weights on the deltas are simply gamma lambda to the power l. In practice, we compute it with one backward pass over the batch: one line of code. Here lambda is zero: we get delta zero, the TD estimate. [click] Lambda equal to one: all the deltas count, we get Monte Carlo. [click] The PPO paper uses lambda equal to 0.95: almost Monte Carlo, but the far future is damped. You can move the slider to see the weights change.

$\lambda$ trades bias for variance

2000 episodes starting with $(S_0, A_0) = (2, \rightarrow)$, same policy and lagging critic as before. Target: $q_\pi(S_0,A_0) - \vhat(S_0,\bw)$ (a baseline that depends only on the state does not bias the policy gradient)

Biais–variance de GAE sur 2000 épisodes : λ = 0 sans variance ici (transitions déterministes) mais biaisé (et même de mauvais signe) car le critique est en retard ; λ = 1 sans biais mais très variable ; l'erreur quadratique est minimale autour de λ ≈ 0.75 sur cet exemple.
Why not always use Monte Carlo? Let's measure it: two thousand episodes, all starting with the same state and the same action. On the left, the histogram of the advantage estimates; the black line is the target. With lambda equal to zero, there is no variance at all here: transitions are deterministic. But the estimate is biased, because the critic is wrong. It is even on the wrong side of zero. [click] With lambda equal to one, the bias disappears, but the estimates are spread out: high variance. [click] On the right, the bias, the standard deviation, and the total error. The best compromise is in between: here around 0.75. With neural networks and long episodes, people use 0.9 to 0.97.

Algorithm so far: actor-critic with rollouts and GAE

Initialize $\bth$ and $\bw$ ; $S_0 \leftarrow$ first state
Loop for each iteration:
for $t = 0, \dots, T-1$ do   ▷ rollout with the current policy
$A_t \sim \pi(\cdot|S_t,\bth)$ ; take $A_t$, observe $R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}$   ▷ new episode if terminal
$\delta_t \leftarrow R_{t+1} + \gamma \vhat(S_{t+1},\bw) - \vhat(S_t,\bw)$   for all $t$
$\hAdv_t \leftarrow \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,\hAdv_{t+1}$   for $t = T-1, \dots, 0$ ;   $\Vtarg_t \leftarrow \hAdv_t + \vhat(S_t,\bw)$
$\bth \leftarrow \bth + \alpha^{\bth} \, \frac{1}{T} \sum_t \hAdv_t \, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t|S_t,\bth)$
$\bw \leftarrow \bw + \alpha^{\bw} \, \frac{1}{T} \sum_t \big(\Vtarg_t - \vhat(S_t,\bw)\big) \nabla \vhat(S_t,\bw)$
  • $\delta \rightarrow \hAdv_t$, and the critic regresses on $\Vtarg_t$ (the $\lambda$-return) instead of $R + \gamma \vhat(S')$
  • The factor $I = \gamma^t$ is dropped, as in all practical implementations
  • With $N$ parallel environments, this is A2C (Mnih et al., 2016)
  • Still one gradient step per batch: the batch is then thrown away
L'algorithme à ce stade (lignes vertes = rollouts + GAE) : c'est A2C avec GAE. Le facteur I est abandonné, comme dans toutes les implémentations. Un seul pas de gradient par lot.
Let's write down the algorithm so far. The green lines are new. We collect a rollout of T steps, compute the deltas, then the advantages with one backward pass. The critic now regresses on a better target: the lambda-return. Note that we drop the I factor, like every practical implementation. With several environments in parallel, this algorithm is called A2C. But look at the last two lines. We still do one gradient step, and then the batch is thrown away.

Part 2

Reusing the batch: the importance ratio

Partie 2 : réutiliser le lot.
Part two: how to reuse each batch several times.

One gradient step per batch is wasteful

  • Collecting data is the expensive part (simulator, robot, text generation by an LLM…)
  • Idea: do $K$ epochs of minibatch gradient steps on the same batch
  • Problem: after the first step, $\bth \neq \thold$. The batch was sampled by $\pi(\cdot|\cdot,\thold)$, not by the policy we are improving: $\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \hAdv_t \nabla \ln \pi(A_t|S_t,\bth)$ is no longer the policy gradient at $\bth$
reuse the batch
($K$ epochs)
→
off-policy data:
importance ratio $\rho_t(\bth)$
→
maximizing the new objective makes the policy diverge
→
clip $\rho_t(\bth)$

Why not clip TD Actor-Critic directly? Each transition is used once, at $\bth = \thold$: the ratio is always $\rho_t = 1$, and the clip would do nothing. Clipping only makes sense with data reuse.

Réutiliser le lot sur K époques. Problème : après le premier pas, les données viennent de l'ancienne politique. D'où l'enchaînement : réutiliser → ratio d'importance → l'objectif obtenu fait diverger la politique → clip. Dans TD AC, chaque transition sert une seule fois à θ = θ_old : le ratio vaut 1 et le clip ne ferait rien.
Collecting data is the expensive part: a simulator, a real robot, or an LLM generating text. [click] So the idea is natural: do K epochs of gradient steps on the same batch. [click] But after the first step, the data come from the old policy, not from the one we improve. [click] Here is the whole story of PPO: we reuse the batch, so we need an importance ratio. [click] But maximizing the new objective makes the policy diverge. [click] So we clip the ratio. [click] In TD actor-critic, each transition is used once, by the old policy itself. The ratio is always one: the clip would do nothing.

Importance sampling: the surrogate objective

Importance sampling:   $\E_{A \sim \pi(\cdot|s,\bth)}\big[f(A)\big] = \E_{A \sim \pi(\cdot|s,\thold)}\Big[\frac{\pi(A|s,\bth)}{\pi(A|s,\thold)} \, f(A)\Big]$

Importance ratio (Sutton & Barto, ch. 5 and 7):   $\rho_t(\bth) \doteq \dfrac{\pi(A_t|S_t,\bth)}{\pi(A_t|S_t,\thold)}$,   with $\rho_t(\thold) = 1$

Surrogate objective ("conservative policy iteration", Kakade & Langford, 2002), $\Ehat_t$ = average over the batch: $$\Lcpi(\bth) \doteq \Ehat_t\big[\rho_t(\bth) \, \hAdv_t\big]$$

At $\bth = \thold$:   $\nabla \rho_t(\bth)\big|_{\thold} = \dfrac{\nabla \pi(A_t|S_t,\thold)}{\pi(A_t|S_t,\thold)} = \nabla \ln \pi(A_t|S_t,\thold)$ $$\Rightarrow \quad \nabla \Lcpi(\thold) = \Ehat_t\big[\hAdv_t \, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t|S_t,\thold)\big] \quad \text{(the A2C gradient)}$$

The first step on $\Lcpi$ is exactly the A2C step; the next steps correct for the change of policy… but only for the actions, not for the states: the next states would also change.

Échantillonnage préférentiel : ratio ρ_t(θ) (notation Sutton & Barto ; r_t(θ) dans le papier PPO). Objectif de substitution L^CPI. Au point θ_old, son gradient est exactement celui d'A2C. Il corrige le changement de loi des actions, pas celui des états.
The classic tool for off-policy data is importance sampling. An expectation under the new policy is an expectation under the old one, reweighted by the ratio of probabilities. [click] This ratio is called rho t, the importance ratio, as in Sutton and Barto. At theta old, it is equal to one. [click] So we maximize the average of rho times the advantage: the surrogate objective. The name CPI comes from Kakade and Langford, in 2002. [click] Look at its gradient at theta old. The gradient of rho is the gradient of log pi. So the first step is exactly the A2C step. [click] The next steps correct for the change of policy, but only for the actions. The states would also change with a new policy, and the surrogate ignores it.

The surrogate is only valid near $\thold$

Performance difference lemma (Kakade & Langford, 2002), for any two policies: $$J(\bth) - J(\thold) = \E_{\pi_{\bth}}\Big[\sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t \Adv_{\pi_{\thold}}(S_t, A_t)\Big]$$ Proof: $\Adv_{\pi_{\thold}}(S_t,A_t) = \E\big[R_{t+1} + \gamma v_{\pi_{\thold}}(S_{t+1}) - v_{\pi_{\thold}}(S_t)\big]$, and the sum telescopes to $G_0 - v_{\pi_{\thold}}(s_0)$.

With importance sampling on the actions only: $$J(\bth) - J(\thold) = \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t \, \E_{\color{#ee6a6a}{S_t \sim \pi_{\bth}}} \, \E_{A_t \sim \pi_{\thold}}\big[\rho_t(\bth) \, \Adv_{\pi_{\thold}}(S_t,A_t)\big]$$

The surrogate replaces ${\color{#ee6a6a}{S_t \sim \pi_{\bth}}}$ by $S_t \sim \pi_{\thold}$ — the states we have — and drops $\gamma^t$ (like $I$): $$J(\bth) - J(\thold) \approx \Lcpi(\bth) \qquad \text{good approximation only if } \pi_{\bth} \text{ stays close to } \pi_{\thold}$$

Far from $\thold$, the surrogate can promise an improvement that does not exist. How far can we go?   → trust regions (TRPO), then clipping (PPO).

Lemme de différence de performance (Kakade & Langford) : trajectoires de la nouvelle politique, avantages de l'ancienne. L'objectif de substitution remplace la loi des états de la nouvelle politique par celle de l'ancienne : il n'est valable que près de θ_old.
How wrong is the surrogate? There is an exact answer. The performance difference lemma says: the gain of a new policy is the sum of the advantages of the old policy, along trajectories of the new one. The proof is short: the sum of TD errors telescopes. [click] With importance sampling, we can sample the actions from the old policy. But the states, in red, still come from the new policy. [click] The surrogate simply uses the states we have, from the old policy. So it is a good approximation only if the new policy stays close to the old one. [click] Far away, it can promise an improvement that does not exist. So, how far can we go? Let's look at a concrete example.

Maximizing the surrogate: what goes wrong

One-state toy problem: action $a \in \R$, policy $\pi(a|\bth) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\bth = (\mu, \ln\sigma)$. 12 actions from $\pi(\cdot|\thold) = \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, noisy rewards, $\hAdv_i = r_i - \overline{r}$. 10 epochs of gradient ascent on $\Lcpi$.

$\Lcpi$ is unbounded: all the probability on the best sample gives $\rho \to \infty$. $\sigma$ collapses, the gradients explode, the policy jumps away: $J$ ends below its starting value.

Bandit gaussien à un état : 10 époques de montée de gradient sur L^CPI, sur un même lot de 12 actions. J s'améliore d'abord, puis σ s'effondre, les ratios explosent, la politique saute loin et J finit sous sa valeur initiale.
To see the problem, we use the simplest possible example: a problem with one state. The action is a real number, and the policy is a Gaussian. In gray, the old policy, and the twelve actions it sampled. Each sample has an advantage: blue if positive, red if negative. In yellow, the true mean reward, that the agent does not know. [click × 7: epochs 1 to 7] At first, all is well: the policy moves right, toward the good samples, and the true gain J increases. But sigma shrinks, the ratios explode, and the surrogate promises much more than the true gain. [click] At epoch 8, the gradient explodes, and the policy jumps far away. [click × 2] Now the true gain is negative: we are worse than before the update. [click] The surrogate is unbounded. Putting all the probability on the best sample makes rho go to infinity.

Part 3

Clipping: small policy steps

Partie 3 : le clipping.
Part three: how to keep each policy step small, with clipping.

Stay close to $\thold$: trust regions (TRPO)

Lower bound (Schulman et al., 2015): with $C = \frac{4\gamma \max_{s,a}|\Adv_{\pi_{\thold}}(s,a)|}{(1-\gamma)^2}$, $$J(\bth) \;\ge\; J(\thold) + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\thold}(\bth)}_{\text{surrogate gain}} - \; C \, \max_s \KL\big(\pi(\cdot|s,\thold) \,\|\, \pi(\cdot|s,\bth)\big)$$ Maximizing the right-hand side at each iteration gives a monotonic improvement (in theory).

TRPO, the practical version: a hard constraint on the average KL divergence, $$\max_{\bth} \; \Lcpi(\bth) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \Ehat_t\Big[\KL\big(\pi(\cdot|S_t,\thold) \,\|\, \pi(\cdot|S_t,\bth)\big)\Big] \le \kappa \qquad (\kappa \approx 0.01)$$

  • Solved with a natural gradient step: Fisher matrix, conjugate gradient, line search
  • Second-order and complex; does not fit well with shared actor-critic networks or dropout

PPO's question: the same effect, with plain first-order SGD (Adam) on an unconstrained objective? The paper proposes an adaptive KL penalty and a clipped objective; the clipped one is the PPO everybody uses.

Borne inférieure de TRPO : gain de substitution moins C fois le KL maximal. TRPO : contrainte de KL moyen, résolue au second ordre (gradient naturel, gradient conjugué, recherche linéaire). PPO cherche le même effet avec un simple SGD.
The theory gives a precise answer. Schulman and colleagues proved a lower bound on the true performance. It is the surrogate gain, minus a penalty on the KL divergence between the old and the new policy. If we maximize this bound at each iteration, the performance can only improve. [click] TRPO turns this into a constraint: maximize the surrogate, but keep the average KL below a small threshold. This region around theta old is the trust region. [click] It works well, but it is a second-order method: Fisher matrix, conjugate gradient, line search. It is complex, and hard to combine with modern network architectures. [click] So PPO asks a simple question: can we get the same effect with plain SGD?

The clipped objective

$\Lclip(\bth) \doteq \Ehat_t\Big[\min\big(\rho_t(\bth) \, \hAdv_t, \; {\color{#cc79a7}{\clip\big(\rho_t(\bth), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\big)}} \, \hAdv_t\big)\Big]$   ($\varepsilon = 0.2$)

  • Beyond $1 \pm \varepsilon$ in the good direction: the term is flat, its gradient is zero → no incentive to move further
  • In the bad direction, the $\min$ keeps the full penalty: a pessimistic bound, $\Lclip \le \Lcpi$, equal near $\rho = 1$
L^CLIP = min(ρ Adv, clip(ρ, 1−ε, 1+ε) Adv). Avantage positif : aucun gain au-delà de 1+ε ; négatif : aucun gain en dessous de 1−ε. Le min rend l'objectif pessimiste : la pénalité reste entière si ρ part dans le mauvais sens. Curseur pour ε.
Here is PPO's answer: the clipped objective. Each term of the surrogate is a function of the ratio rho. On the left, a sample with a positive advantage; on the right, a negative one. [click] Without clipping, the term is a line: rho times the advantage. For a good action, it grows without limit. [click] The clipped term, in purple, stops at one plus epsilon, or one minus epsilon. [click] PPO takes the minimum of the two: the thick black curve. [click] In the gray zones, the curve is flat: the gradient is zero. Once the probability of a good action has grown by twenty percent, there is no incentive to push further. But if the ratio went the wrong way, the full penalty stays: the objective is pessimistic.

Clipping in action

Same batch, same optimizer, same 10 epochs; only the objective changes: $\Lclip$ instead of $\Lcpi$. A hollow sample is clipped: it no longer contributes to the gradient.

A smaller but safe step: the next batch will continue. (Not a hard constraint: other samples can push a ratio out of the band; Wang et al., 2019.)
PPO paper, MuJoCo, normalized score: no clipping $-0.39$; clipping with $\varepsilon = 0.2$: $0.82$.

Même lot, même optimiseur, même nombre d'époques, avec L^CLIP : les échantillons clippés (évidés) ne contribuent plus au gradient. Pas plus petit mais sûr. Le clip n'est pas une contrainte dure. Table 1 du papier PPO : −0.39 sans clip, 0.82 avec ε = 0.2.
Now the same experiment: same batch, same optimizer, same ten epochs. Only the objective changes: we use the clipped one. [click] The first epoch is identical: the ratios are all equal to one. [click × 9: epochs 2 to 10] Now look at the samples that become hollow. Their ratio left the band, in the good direction: they stop pushing. The policy moves a little, then almost stops: the clipped surrogate becomes flat. The true gain stays positive, and the policy keeps its spread. [click] It is a smaller step, but a safe one: the next batch will continue the work. And on real benchmarks, the difference is huge. In the PPO paper, without clipping the normalized score is minus 0.39: worse than the random initial policy. With clipping, it is 0.82.

Why clipping only matters with data reuse

Ratios $\rho_t(\bth)$ of the 16 transitions of one batch in the corridor, at the start of each PPO epoch ($\varepsilon = 0.2$).

  • First epoch: $\bth = \thold$, so all $\rho_t = 1$ and $\clip(\rho_t) = \rho_t$ — this is all that TD Actor-Critic and A2C ever see
  • The clip acts only from the second pass over the same data: it is the safeguard of data reuse
Ratios des 16 transitions d'un lot du couloir au début de chaque époque : à la première époque, tous valent 1 (c'est tout ce que voient TD AC et A2C) ; le clip n'agit qu'à partir de la deuxième passe sur les mêmes données.
Let's come back to the corridor, to see when the clip really acts. Each column is one transition of a batch, each row is one epoch of PPO. The color is the ratio rho: white means one. At the first epoch, all the ratios are exactly one. This is all that TD actor-critic and A2C ever see: the clip would do nothing. [click × 2: epochs 2 and 3] From the second epoch, the ratios move away from one. A circled cell is clipped: its gradient is zero. [click × 2] The more we reuse the batch, the more samples are clipped. So the clip is the safeguard of data reuse. The two ingredients go together.

Part 4

The full loss, and PPO step by step

Partie 4 : la fonction objectif complète, puis PPO pas à pas.
Part four: the full objective of PPO, and then the whole algorithm, step by step.

One objective, three terms

$$L(\bth, \bw) = \Ehat_t\Big[\, {\color{#cc79a7}{\Lclip_t(\bth)}} \; - \; c_1 \, {\color{#555}{\big(\vhat(S_t,\bw) - \Vtarg_t\big)^2}} \; + \; c_2 \, {\color{#0072b2}{\mathcal{H}\big(\pi(\cdot|S_t,\bth)\big)}} \,\Big]$$

  • Actor: the clipped surrogate, just seen
  • Critic: the squared error on $\Vtarg_t = \hAdv_t + \vhat(S_t,\bw_{\text{old}})$ — already in TD Actor-Critic. Written as a loss so that one optimizer can train a network shared by the actor and the critic. With two separate networks, $c_1$ is irrelevant.
  • Entropy bonus: $\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|s)) = -\sum_a \pi(a|s) \ln \pi(a|s)$ rewards stochastic policies → keeps exploring

PPO paper: $c_1 = 1$, $c_2 = 0.01$ on Atari; on MuJoCo, separate networks and no entropy bonus ($c_2 = 0$). The paper maximizes $L$; libraries minimize $-L$.

Objectif complet : terme clippé (acteur), erreur quadratique du critique (déjà dans TD AC, écrite comme une perte pour un réseau partagé), bonus d'entropie. Papier : c₁ = 1, c₂ = 0.01 sur Atari ; pas d'entropie sur MuJoCo.
In practice, PPO optimizes one objective with three terms. [click] The first one is the clipped surrogate, for the actor. [click] The second one is the critic: a squared error on the lambda-return. This is nothing new: the critic was already in TD actor-critic. We write it as a loss because, very often, the actor and the critic share the same network. [click] The third one is new: an entropy bonus. It rewards policies that stay random, so that the agent keeps exploring. [click] How important is it? In the PPO paper, it is used on Atari, but not at all on MuJoCo. So it is a secondary ingredient. But let's see what it can do.

Entropy bonus: keep exploring

Same one-state problem, but the reward has two modes: a small one nearby, a big one far away. PPO (clip), 60 iterations of 16 actions; for a Gaussian, $\mathcal{H} = \ln\sigma + \text{const}$.

Bonus d'entropie sur un bandit à deux modes (100 seeds par valeur de c₂) : sans entropie, σ s'effondre sur le mode local ; avec c₂ = 0.05, la plupart des exécutions trouvent le mode global ; avec c₂ trop grand, σ diverge et la politique ne s'engage jamais. Curseur pour c₂.
Back to our one-state problem, with a harder reward: a small mode nearby, and a big mode far away. On the left, one run of PPO: each row is the policy at one iteration, from top to bottom. On the right, the results of a hundred runs for each value of c 2. Without entropy bonus, sigma shrinks fast, and the policy is trapped on the small mode. Only about one run in five finds the big one. [click] With c 2 equal to 0.05, the policy stays wide long enough to discover the big mode. Now most runs find it. [click] But too much entropy is bad too: sigma grows without limit, and the policy never commits. So c 2 is a real hyperparameter, with a sweet spot.

PPO, step by step

Initialize $\bth$ and $\bw$ ; $S_0 \leftarrow$ first state
for iteration $= 1, 2, \dots$ do
$\thold \leftarrow \bth$
for $t = 0, \dots, T-1$ do
$A_t \sim \pi(\cdot|S_t,\thold)$ ; observe $R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}$
$\delta_t \leftarrow R_{t+1} + \gamma \vhat(S_{t+1},\bw) - \vhat(S_t,\bw)$
$\hAdv_t \leftarrow \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,\hAdv_{t+1}$   ▷ $t = T-1, \dots, 0$
$\Vtarg_t \leftarrow \hAdv_t + \vhat(S_t,\bw)$
for epoch $= 1, \dots, K$ do
for each minibatch $B$ do
$\rho_t \leftarrow \pi(A_t|S_t,\bth) \,/\, \pi(A_t|S_t,\thold)$
$\Lclip \leftarrow \Ehat_B\big[\min\big(\rho_t \hAdv_t, \clip(\rho_t, 1 \pm \varepsilon)\hAdv_t\big)\big]$
$L \leftarrow \Lclip - c_1 \Ehat_B\big[\big(\vhat(S_t,\bw) - \Vtarg_t\big)^2\big]$
$\phantom{L \leftarrow} + \, c_2 \Ehat_B\big[\mathcal{H}\big(\pi(\cdot|S_t,\bth)\big)\big]$
$(\bth, \bw) \leftarrow (\bth, \bw) + \alpha \nabla L$   ▷ Adam in practice

Here: tabular actor and critic, $T = 8$, $\lambda = 0.95$, $K = 3$ epochs of 2 minibatches, $\varepsilon = 0.2$, $c_2 = 0$, plain SGD.

PPO complet, pas à pas sur le couloir (T = 8, K = 3 époques de 2 minibatches, ε = 0.2) : collecte, δ, GAE à rebours (le signal de récompense remonte toute la trajectoire en une passe), puis les époques avec ratios et échantillons clippés, puis nouvelle itération.
Here is the complete algorithm, with the colors of the four ingredients. On the right, it runs on the corridor, with a buffer of only eight steps. [click × 9: rollout] First, the rollout: we play the old policy for eight steps, and we fill the buffer. [click × 2] Then the deltas, and the advantages, computed backwards. Look: the reward signal goes back along the whole trajectory in one pass, not one state per visit. [click × 6: three epochs of two minibatches] Then the epochs: at the first minibatch, all the ratios are one. Then they move away, and some samples get clipped. The arrows of the policy change a little at each step. [click] Then theta old becomes theta, the batch is thrown away, and we start a new iteration. [click] After thirty iterations, the agent goes right: it found the big reward.

Does it work? Four variants on the corridor

Courbes d'apprentissage sur 100 seeds : A2C (une époque) lent ; réutilisation naïve (10 époques sans clip) rapide mais souvent piégée à gauche ; PPO (clip) plus fiable ; PPO + entropie presque toujours à droite.
Does all this work? Let's compare four variants on the corridor, with one hundred seeds each. The y axis is the true performance; the top line is the optimum, going right, and the lower line is the trap, going left. First, A2C: one gradient step per batch. It learns, but slowly. [click] Now naive reuse: ten epochs per batch, without clipping. It learns much faster, but many runs get stuck on the left. [click] With clipping: just as fast, and fewer runs get stuck. Implementation details that matter

  • Normalize the advantages in each minibatch
  • Normalize observations (and scale rewards)
  • Clip the gradient norm (e.g. 0.5); anneal the step size
  • Orthogonal initialization, small last layer for the policy
  • Optionally clip the value loss too
  • Monitor the KL and the fraction of clipped samples

Engstrom et al. (2020): a large part of PPO's advantage over TRPO comes from such code-level choices. Huang et al. (2022) list 37 implementation details.

PPO and large language models

  • RLHF (InstructGPT, Ouyang et al., 2022): the LLM is the policy
    • state = prompt + tokens generated so far; action = next token
    • reward = reward-model score at the end, minus a per-token penalty $\beta \ln \frac{\pi(A_t|S_t,\bth)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(A_t|S_t)}$ that keeps the model close to the reference one
    • PPO with a value head as critic, GAE, clipping
  • GRPO (Shao et al., 2024; DeepSeek-R1): PPO without a critic
    • sample $G$ answers for the same prompt; $\hAdv_i = (r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r)) / \operatorname{std}(r)$
    • keeps the clipped ratio, with a KL term to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in the objective
    • the group baseline is exactly what our Gaussian toy problem used: $\hAdv_i = r_i - \overline{r}$

More in the deck Reinforcement Learning and LLMs.

PPO dans les LLM : RLHF (InstructGPT) avec pénalité KL par token vers le modèle de référence ; GRPO supprime le critique et utilise une ligne de base de groupe, comme notre bandit.
PPO is also at the heart of the training of large language models. In RLHF, as in InstructGPT, the language model is the policy. The state is the prompt plus the tokens generated so far, and the action is the next token. The reward comes from a reward model, with a KL penalty that keeps the model close to the original one. [click] More recently, GRPO, used for DeepSeek R1, removes the critic. For each prompt, it samples a group of answers, and uses the mean reward of the group as baseline. This is exactly what our Gaussian toy problem did. And it keeps the clipped ratio of PPO. [click] We cover this in detail in the deck on reinforcement learning and LLMs.

Take-home messages

IngredientProblem it solvesHow
TD Actor-Criticstarting point$\delta_t$ estimates $\Adv_\pi(S_t,A_t)$
Rollouts + GAE$\delta_t$ is myopic and biased$\hAdv_t = \sum_l (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}$, one backward pass
Data reuseone gradient step per batch$K$ epochs on $\Ehat_t[\rho_t(\bth)\hAdv_t]$
Clippingthe surrogate makes the policy diverge$\min\big(\rho_t\hAdv_t, \clip(\rho_t, 1 \pm \varepsilon)\hAdv_t\big)$
Entropypremature convergence$+\, c_2 \, \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|S_t,\bth))$

The key chain: rollouts give a batch → we reuse it → data are off-policy, so we need $\rho_t(\bth)$ → maximizing the surrogate makes the policy diverge → we clip $\rho_t(\bth)$. Without data reuse, $\rho_t = 1$ and clipping does nothing.

Messages à retenir : chaque ingrédient, le problème qu'il résout, et comment. L'enchaînement clé : réutiliser le lot → ratio → divergence → clip ; sans réutilisation, le clip ne fait rien.
To conclude, PPO is TD actor-critic plus four ingredients. Rollouts and GAE give better advantage estimates, with one backward pass. Data reuse gives several epochs per batch, thanks to the importance ratio. Clipping keeps each policy step small and safe. And an entropy bonus keeps the policy exploring. The most important idea is the chain in the middle. We reuse the batch, so we need a ratio, so we need a clip. Without data reuse, rho t = 1 and clipping does nothing.
